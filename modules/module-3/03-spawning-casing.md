# Module 3: Aseptic Spawning, Spawn Run, & Casing Science

> **Learning outcomes.** You will be able to (1) select and source spawn and protect it through a Kenyan road cold chain, (2) spawn and fill compost cleanly at the correct rate, (3) hold the spawn run inside its narrow thermal window in highland conditions, and (4) formulate, buffer, pasteurise, and apply a casing layer from locally available materials.
>
> **Linked documents:** SOP-301 · SOP-302 · Deliverable-303 · Deliverable-101 · KE-00 §2.2–2.3

## 1. Strain Selection

| Strain type | Characteristics | Market fit (Kenya) | Market fit (global) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Smooth white hybrid** (U1-derived commercial lines) | High yield, white smooth cap, bruises visibly | Supermarket punnets (Naivas, Quickmart, Carrefour), hotels; the dominant Kenyan demand | EU/USA fresh market staple |
| **Off-white / cream** | Sturdier cap, slightly better shelf life, more tolerant of handling | Open-air wholesale markets, longer-haul routes (Eldoret → Kisumu/Kampala) | Processing and canning |
| **Brown (chestnut / cremini → portobello)** | Lower yield (≈ 10–20% less), better shelf life, richer flavour | Premium hotels, restaurants, upmarket retail. Niche but higher price | Growing specialty segment |

**Strain selection rules:**
1. Trial a new strain on one room before switching the whole farm.
2. Keep a strain log with yields per flush and disease incidence (Deliverable-404).
3. Ask suppliers for the strain's **temperature profile**. Some white hybrids tolerate the 18–19 °C fruiting temperatures that Kenyan passive rooms reach on warm afternoons better than others.

## 2. Spawn Sourcing and the Kenyan Cold Chain

### 2.1 Supply options

* **Local spawn laboratories** (Juja/Nairobi/Nakuru area and other university or private labs): shortest cold chain, local payment, faster reorders. **Audit the lab** before your first order: clean-room practice, sterilisation logs, mother-culture renewal, and a written purity guarantee.
* **Imported spawn** (European or South African spawn houses): consistent genetics. It needs a **KEPHIS plant import permit** and phytosanitary certificate, refrigerated air freight, and clearance at JKIA within hours.

### 2.2 Spawn biology that drives logistics

Grain spawn is alive and respiring. Above ≈ 8 °C it generates heat; packed tightly in a warm vehicle, the core of a spawn box can rise past 30 °C and kill or weaken the mycelium and favour bacteria. **Cold, ventilated, and not over-stacked** is the rule.

### 2.3 Road transport protocol (lab → farm)

| Step | Requirement | Kenyan field practice |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Packaging** | Spawn bags in rigid crates/boxes, not loose | Plastic crates protect bags from vibration damage on murram roads |
| **Cooling** | Hold 2–8 °C (ideal 2–4 °C), **never frozen** | Quality insulated cooler boxes with frozen gel packs **wrapped in cardboard or newspaper** (direct contact freezes grain). Ratio ≈ 1 kg of gel pack per 3–4 kg of spawn for trips ≤ 8 h |
| **Monitoring** | Logged temperature | A cheap USB/Bluetooth logger in every consignment; reject or quarantine if > 15 °C for > 4 h |
| **Timing** | Travel in cool hours | Depart at dawn; avoid midday traffic on the Nairobi bypasses/Thika Road; never leave coolers in a parked car in the sun |
| **Vehicle** | Shaded, ventilated | Inside the cab or a covered pickup with a tarp shade and airflow. **Never on a matatu roof rack.** For long hauls (Eldoret, Nyeri, Timau), use refrigerated courier or overnight bus parcel services with gel-pack coolers rated ≥ 18 h |
| **Hand-over** | Immediate refrigeration | Farm fridge or cold room at 2–4 °C on arrival; record arrival temperature on the delivery note |

### 2.4 Spawn receiving inspection

* [ ] Uniform, bright white mycelium through every grain; no dry or uncolonised patches
* [ ] **No** green, black, yellow, or pink spots (*Trichoderma*, *Penicillium*, *Neurospora*, other moulds)
* [ ] **No** sour, fruity, or rotten smell and no wet or greasy grains (bacterial "wet spot", *Bacillus*)
* [ ] Not over-aged: minimal clumping, no pins or primordia, no yellow exudate
* [ ] Bag integrity intact; label shows strain, lot number, and production date
* [ ] Arrival temperature recorded ≤ 8 °C

**Reject or quarantine any failing bag. Contaminated spawn infects the entire batch of compost it is mixed into.**

### 2.5 Storage

2–4 °C, stacked with air gaps, used within the supplier's stated shelf life (typically 4–8 weeks). Bring spawn out 6–12 h before use **without opening the bags**, so condensation forms on the outside of the bag rather than on the grain.

## 3. Spawning & Filling

### 3.1 Inoculation rate

* **0.5% – 1.0% of the wet compost weight.** Most commercial farms use ≈ 0.6–0.8%.
* *Example:* 9,000 kg of compost × 0.7% = **63 kg of spawn**.
* Higher rates (towards 1.0%) shorten the spawn run and help colonisation outrun competitors. That is worth paying for in the **rainy seasons**, when contamination pressure is high, or when compost quality is uncertain.

### 3.2 Methods

| Method | Description | Typical context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Through-spawning, bulk** | Spawn mixed through the compost on a clean conveyor or mixer, then filled | Industrial (EU/USA), Kenyan Track K2 |
| **Through-spawning, manual** | Compost spread on a disinfected concrete floor or plastic sheet, spawn broadcast and mixed with clean forks in a sanitised spawning hall | Kenyan Track K1 |
| **Layer spawning** | Spawn sprinkled between 3–4 compost layers in the bag or tray | Small farms. Slower and less uniform; use only when mixing space is lacking |

### 3.3 Container systems and fill density

| System | Fill | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Polythene bags** (tubular, ≈ 40–50 cm diameter, 10–20 kg) | ≈ 85–100 kg/m² of shelf area | The Kenyan standard for passive farms. Fold tops loosely during spawn run; open and level the surface before casing |
| **Wooden trays/beds** | ≈ 80–100 kg/m², 15–20 cm deep | Use treated hardwood or eucalyptus and pasteurise between crops |
| **Dutch aluminium shelves** | 85–100 kg/m², machine-filled | Industrial benchmark; nets pulled for emptying |
| **Phase III blocks** (hub-and-spoke supply) | Pre-colonised compost | Shortens a grower's room cycle by ≈ 2–3 weeks |

### 3.4 Spawning hygiene essentials (detail in SOP-301)

* Clean before dirty: spawning happens in a room cleaned and disinfected **after** the previous use.
* Staff in clean overalls, hair covered, hands washed and sanitised, boots through a footbath.
* All tools, conveyors, and forks disinfected; no compost dust tracked in from the yard.
* Fill, close, and record each bag or tray batch with room, date, and lot number.

## 4. Spawn Run (Colonisation)

### 4.1 Environmental window

| Parameter | Target | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Compost core temperature | **24–25 °C** | Fastest healthy growth |
| Absolute ceiling | **27 °C**; alarm at 26.5 °C | Above ≈ 27 °C the mycelium is damaged and competitors (*Trichoderma*, thermophilic moulds) gain the advantage. Above ≈ 30 °C the damage is severe and irreversible |
| Floor | ≥ 22 °C | Below this the run slows sharply and competitors catch up |
| Room air | 20–23 °C | The compost's own heat supplies the difference |
| RH | 90–95% | Prevents surface drying |
| CO₂ | 5,000–10,000 ppm | Promotes vegetative growth and suppresses premature pinning |
| Duration | **14–18 days** to full colonisation | Monitor visually: compost should turn evenly grey-white |

### 4.2 Highland thermal management

Compost heat output peaks around **days 4–10** of the spawn run. On a Kenyan highland farm that means two opposite risks in the same room:

* **Warm days (Jan–Feb, Sep–Oct) + peak compost heat → overheating.** Responses, in order: open the bag tops slightly, increase recirculation, run night-time fresh air (cold highland night air is your free chiller), space bags further apart (≥ 5 cm), reduce bag size for the next batch (10–12 kg instead of 18–20 kg).
* **Cold nights (Jun–Aug, high sites) → stalling.** Responses: close vents fully at night, check door seals, insulate, and add thermostatic electric heating (Module 1 §4.4). Never use charcoal or paraffin heaters.

Log the compost core temperature at least **twice daily** (06:00–08:00 and 15:00–17:00, the coldest and hottest points of the Kenyan highland day) on **Deliverable-303**.

### 4.3 Readiness for casing

* [ ] Compost fully colonised; no uncolonised dark pockets
* [ ] Sweet mushroom smell; no ammonia or sourness
* [ ] No green, olive, or pink patches (if found, see SOP-402)
* [ ] Surface levelled and lightly firmed

## 5. Casing Layer Science

### 5.1 What casing does

*A. bisporus* will not fruit on compost alone. The casing layer is a nutrient-poor, water-holding, well-aerated cover that:
1. Holds the water reservoir that fruit bodies draw on (a mushroom is ~90% water).
2. Creates the CO₂ gradient (high at the compost, low at the surface) that triggers pinning.
3. Hosts beneficial bacteria (*Pseudomonas putida* and others) that consume the mycelium's volatile inhibitors and help induce fruiting.

### 5.2 Casing property targets

| Property | Target |
| :--- | :--- |
| pH | **7.2 – 7.5** (buffered with agricultural limestone, CaCO₃) |
| Water-holding capacity | High: material should hold ≥ 2–3× its dry weight in water |
| Structure | Crumb, open; must not seal or crust when watered |
| EC (salts) | < 1.0 mS/cm (1:5 water extract) |
| Nutrients | Low (casing must not feed competitors) |
| Free of pathogens | Pasteurised: no *Mycogone*, *Verticillium*, nematodes, fly larvae |

### 5.3 Kenyan casing formulations

| Formula | Composition (by volume) | Lime requirement (start point, confirm by jar test) | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **CS-KE1 "Coir–Loam"** | 50% washed/buffered coco peat + 50% red volcanic subsoil (40–80 cm depth, sieved ≤ 10 mm) | ≈ 15–25 kg CaCO₃ per m³ | Best all-round local option. Coir adds water holding; subsoil adds structure and buffering |
| **CS-KE2 "Sandy-Loam Mix"** | 60% sandy loam + 25% coco peat + 15% weathered SMC | ≈ 10–20 kg/m³ | Where good loam is available (e.g., parts of Nakuru/Naivasha); check salt levels of the SMC |
| **CS-KE3 "Peat–Coir Premium"** | 60% imported sphagnum peat + 40% buffered coco peat | ≈ 25–40 kg/m³ (peat is very acidic, pH 3.5–4.5) | Highest consistency; for premium or export crops |
| **CS-GL "Global Standard"** | Black peat / sphagnum peat + sugar-beet lime or limestone | Per supplier | EU/USA standard; often bought ready-made |

**Do not use** black cotton soil (it seals and cracks), unpasteurised topsoil (*Mycogone* wet bubble and nematodes), or forest/wetland material extracted from protected areas (illegal under KFS/EMCA rules; see KE-00 §2.2).

### 5.4 pH buffering — the jar test (lime titration)

The lime requirement of Kenyan subsoils varies widely (red Kiambu soils at pH 4.5–5.5 need far more than Rift Valley sandy loams at pH 6.5–7.5). Never guess:

1. Prepare five 1-litre samples of the moist casing mix.
2. Add limestone at 0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 g per litre (= 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 kg/m³). Mix thoroughly.
3. Moisten, cover, and leave **48–72 h** (limestone reacts slowly).
4. Measure pH with a calibrated meter (1 part casing : 5 parts distilled water, stir, settle 30 min).
5. Choose the lowest rate that gives **pH 7.2–7.5**, then add ≈ 10% as a reserve: watering with acidic rainwater and acid by-products of the mycelium lower pH during cropping.
6. **High-alkalinity borehole water** (KE-00 §3.4) pushes pH up over the crop, so use the lower end of the range.

### 5.5 Pasteurisation

* **Steam:** 60–65 °C throughout the mass for **4–6 hours**, then cool. In the Kenyan K1 track, use the Phase II chamber between compost batches or a dedicated small steam box.
* **Solarisation is not a substitute.** Black-polythene solar heating rarely holds 60 °C throughout the mass for long enough and leaves cold pockets.
* Store pasteurised casing **covered** in a clean area; use within 3–5 days.

### 5.6 Application

1. Bring casing moisture to near field capacity (squeeze: holds shape, glistens, one or two drops).
2. Apply a uniform **3–5 cm** layer (thinner on bags with less compost, thicker on deep trays). Uneven depth gives uneven pinning.
3. Water in lightly over 2–3 days, building to field capacity, without letting water run through into the compost.
4. **Case run:** 7–10 days at 22–24 °C air (compost 23–25 °C), RH 90–95%, CO₂ 3,000–5,000 ppm, until mycelium shows at the casing surface across ~70% of the area.
5. **Ruffling (scratching):** around day 5–7 of the case run, rake the top 2–3 cm to redistribute the mycelium. This gives more uniform, denser pinning. Clean the rakes between rooms.
6. **Advanced (industrial): CACing.** Mixing ≈ 0.5–1% colonised compost into the casing ("compost added at casing") speeds the case run by 2–3 days and evens out pinning. Only do this with clean, fully colonised compost.
