## 1. Agro-Climatic Siting Data

### 1.1 Why the Kenyan highlands suit *Agaricus bisporus*

*A. bisporus* needs two very different thermal regimes:

| Stage | Required temperature | What the Kenyan highlands provide |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Spawn run (vegetative) | Compost 24–25 °C (never > 27 °C) | Compost is **self-heating** (exothermic); room air only needs to be 20–23 °C. Highland afternoons supply this; cold nights must be blocked by insulation. |
| Pinning & fruiting | Air 16–18 °C | Mean annual temperature at 1,900–2,300 m is 15–18 °C — the fruiting target **is the local daily mean**. A building with enough thermal mass converts the daily swing into a near-constant mean. |

This is the core engineering insight of the Kenya track: **at the right altitude you do not need to manufacture cold — you only need to average out the day/night swing.** In Europe and the USA the same result requires chillers; in coastal West Africa it requires either chillers or a warm-tolerant species.

**Altitude rule of thumb (environmental lapse rate):** air temperature falls by roughly 0.6 °C per 100 m of elevation gain. Moving a site from 1,500 m (e.g., Thika) to 2,200 m (e.g., Limuru) lowers mean temperature by about 4 °C — the difference between needing a chiller and not needing one.

### 1.3 Seasonal calendar and operational consequences

| Period | Climate signature | Production risk | Operational response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Jan – Feb** (hot, dry) | Highest afternoon maxima, low RH, strong sun | Fruiting rooms overheat in afternoons; compost yard dries out fast | Night-flush ventilation; wet hessian curtains (high wet-bulb depression = effective); shade-net compost yard; increase Phase I watering. |
| **Mar – May** (long rains) | High RH, overcast, cooler | Green mould, bacterial blotch, sciarid surges; waterlogged compost yard; road access to farms fails | Roofed yard is mandatory; reduce misting, increase air movement; heightened IPM; schedule straw stocking **before** March. |
| **Jun – Aug** (cool, overcast, "winter") | Coldest nights, drizzle/mist at altitude | Spawn run stalls (compost < 22 °C); slow pinning; condensation drip | Close vents at night, insulate spawn-run rooms, supplementary heat at Timau/Molo-type sites; anti-condensation baffles. |
| **Sep – Oct** (warm, dry) | Second heat window | Same as Jan–Feb | As Jan–Feb. Buy and stack straw for the short rains. |
| **Oct – Dec** (short rains) | Humid, warm afternoons | Blotch, Trichoderma, flies | As Mar–May. December holiday demand peak from hotels and supermarkets — plan crops to harvest **mid-Nov to late Dec**. |

### 1.4 Altitude effects every engineer must apply

| Effect | Value at ~2,200 m vs sea level | Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Air density | ≈ 0.98 kg/m³ vs 1.20 kg/m³ (≈ 80%) | A fan moving a given m³/h carries ~20% less mass of air, so ~20% less heat and moisture. **Upsize fans and coolers ~20–25% over sea-level catalogue sizing.** |
| Barometric pressure | ≈ 780 hPa vs 1,013 hPa | Non-pressure-compensated NDIR CO₂ sensors **under-read** by roughly the pressure ratio (≈ 20–23%). Use pressure-compensated meters or apply the correction: CO₂(true) ≈ reading × 1,013 / P(site). |
| Boiling point of water | ≈ 92–93 °C | Atmospheric steam is cooler than at sea level but still far above the 58–62 °C pasteurisation target; allow longer heat-up times. |
| Solar irradiance | High (clear-sky UV and short-wave are intense near the equator) | Roofs are the dominant heat-gain surface (sun almost overhead). Roof insulation and radiant barriers deliver more benefit per shilling than wall insulation. |

| Source | Notes |
| :--- | :--- |

## 3. Water Security Engineering

### 3.1 Water demand estimate

| Use | Rule of thumb | Example: 2 rooms × 100 m² bed, 1 crop/month |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Phase I pre-wetting & make-up | ≈ 3–4 m³ per tonne of straw (plus 20–30% in dry season) | 3.4 t straw → ≈ 12 m³ per batch |
| Casing watering through cropping | ≈ 25–35 L/m² per crop | 100 m² → ≈ 3 m³ per crop |
| Humidification, floor wetting, cooling curtains | ≈ 0.5–1.5 m³/day | ≈ 30 m³/month |
| Cleaning, footbaths, staff | ≈ 0.5 m³/day | ≈ 15 m³/month |
| **Design total** | — | **≈ 60 m³/month → design 3 m³/day average, 10 m³/day peak (compost days)** |

### 3.2 Source options

| Option | When to use | Approvals | Indicative cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rainwater harvesting** | All sites — primary or supplementary | None for roof harvesting | Gutters + tanks ≈ KES 15–25 per litre of storage capacity |
| **Borehole** | Sites without reliable piped water | Hydrogeological survey by a licensed hydrogeologist; **Water Resources Authority (WRA)** authorisation to drill and permit to abstract; NEMA screening; county approval | Survey ≈ KES 40,000–100,000; drilling & casing ≈ KES 6,000–12,000 per metre; Kiambu/Limuru boreholes are often 150–300 m deep. Budget **KES 1.5–4.0M** all-in. |
| **County / water company supply** | Peri-urban sites | Connection application | Tariff per m³ — rationing common in dry months, so always pair with storage |
| **River/stream abstraction** | Only with permit | WRA abstraction permit; riparian rules | Pumping + filtration; high turbidity and contamination risk |

**Rainwater sizing formula:**

> **Annual harvest (m³)** = Roof area (m²) × Annual rainfall (m) × Runoff coefficient C

where C ≈ 0.8–0.9 for iron-sheet roofs. *Example:* a 500 m² roof at Limuru (R ≈ 1.1 m) yields 500 × 1.1 × 0.85 ≈ 468 m³/yr — about 65% of the example farm's ~720 m³/yr demand. Storage must bridge the Jun–Sep and Jan–Feb dry spells: size tanks for **≥ 60 days of demand** if rainwater is the main source.

### 3.3 Solar pumping sizing (worked example)

> **Hydraulic energy (kWh/day)** = 1,000 × 9.81 × Head H (m) × Daily volume V (m³) ÷ 3,600,000

*Example:* V = 10 m³/day (peak), total dynamic head H = 120 m (80 m pumping water level + 15 m tank stand + 25 m friction/margin):

* E(hydraulic) = 1,000 × 9.81 × 120 × 10 ÷ 3,600,000 ≈ 3.3 kWh/day
* Submersible pump wire-to-water efficiency ≈ 45% → 3.3 / 0.45 ≈ 7.3 kWh/day
* Kenyan highland peak sun hours ≈ 5.0–5.5 h; array derating ≈ 0.8 → 7.3 / (5.0 × 0.8) ≈ 1.8 kWp

**Specify ≈ 2 kWp of PV with a DC/AC solar pump controller and ≥ 1 day of elevated storage** (10 m³ tank on a 6–10 m stand gives gravity pressure for misting and cleaning without a booster pump).

### 3.4 Water quality, pH and alkalinity buffering

| Parameter | Target for mushroom use | Common Kenyan issue | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| pH | 6.5 – 7.8 | Rift Valley/volcanic aquifers often pH 7.8–9.0 | Blend with rainwater; dose food-grade phosphoric or citric acid in a mixing tank to pH 7.0–7.5 (test with a calibrated meter). |
| Total alkalinity (as CaCO₃) | < 200 mg/L | High bicarbonate in Nakuru/Naivasha/Kajiado/Magadi-influenced aquifers | Acid neutralisation (as above). High-bicarbonate water pushes casing pH upward over the crop — reduce casing lime when using it. |
| Sodium / EC | EC < 1.0 mS/cm | Saline boreholes in drier counties | Blend or reverse osmosis for casing water; never use for casing if EC > 2 mS/cm. |
| Iron / manganese | Fe < 0.3 mg/L | Common in deep boreholes | Aeration + sand filter; iron stains caps brown and lowers grade. |
| Fluoride | — (staff drinking water ≤ 1.5 mg/L) | High in much of the Rift Valley | Not a crop issue, but provide treated drinking water for staff. |
| Microbiology | No faecal coliforms; low *Pseudomonas* | Shallow wells, open tanks | Cover tanks; chlorinate casing/misting water (see SOP-402); clean tanks quarterly. |

**Test before you design:** commission a full water analysis (pH, EC, alkalinity, hardness, Na, Fe, Mn, F, nitrate, coliforms) from an accredited lab (e.g., water-sector or university laboratories) as part of the borehole completion report.

## 4. Energy

* **Grid tariff:** Kenya Power small-commercial energy costs, all levies included, are typically **KES 22–32 per kWh**. Read your actual bill, which includes fuel-cost, forex and inflation adjustments.
* **Reliability:** Outages and voltage dips are common in rural feeders. Anything holding live product (cold room, spawn-run fans, Phase II blower) needs **backup**: generator with automatic transfer switch, or a solar-plus-battery inverter sized for the critical load.
* **Biomass for steam:** Use sustainably sourced firewood or briquettes (sawdust, bagasse, coffee husk briquettes are locally made). Keep purchase receipts — transport of charcoal/firewood is regulated.

### 5.1 Tax quick-reference (verify current rates every Finance Act cycle)

| Item | Rule (as last known) | Note for mushroom farms |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
