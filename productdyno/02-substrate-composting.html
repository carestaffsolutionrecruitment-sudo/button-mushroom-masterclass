Module 2: Substrate Engineering — Phase I & Phase II Composting

Learning outcomes. You will be able to (1) formulate a compost recipe from Kenyan raw materials using C:N, nitrogen, and moisture targets, (2) run an outdoor Phase I yard through Kenyan wet and dry seasons, (3) run Phase II pasteurisation and conditioning in an industrial tunnel or a low-cost chamber, and (4) judge whether compost is ready to spawn.

Linked documents: SOP-201 · SOP-202 · Deliverable-202 · KE-00 §2 (sourcing), §3 (water), §5 (NEMA)

1. Why Agaricus Needs Selective Compost

Unlike wood-rotting mushrooms (oyster, shiitake), Agaricus bisporus is a secondary decomposer. It grows on a substrate that thermophilic bacteria, actinomycetes, and fungi have already partly digested. Composting has two jobs:

  1. Nutrition: turn straw cellulose and hemicellulose plus manure nitrogen into a lignin–humus–protein complex that Agaricus can use and most competitors cannot.
  2. Selectivity: remove free ammonia and simple sugars and kill pests, so that when spawn is added, Agaricus is the only organism able to colonise quickly.

Poor compost cannot be rescued later in the crop. Most low yields in Kenya trace back to Phase I and Phase II.

2. Raw Material Formulation

2.1 Formulation targets

Parameter Start of Phase I End of Phase II (spawn-ready)
C:N ratio ≈ 25:1 – 30:1 ≈ 17:1 – 20:1
Total nitrogen (% of dry matter) 1.5 – 1.8% 2.0 – 2.4%
Moisture 70 – 75% 66 – 70%
pH 7.5 – 8.5 (rises with ammonia) 7.2 – 7.8
Ash (% DM) < 25% < 30%
Gypsum 3–5% of total dry ingredient mass (≈ 40–60 kg per tonne of straw) —
Free ammonia High (it drives Phase I) ≤ 5–10 ppm in air, no smell

Audit note — check C:N and N%. A recipe can hit 30:1 and still be nitrogen-starved if the carbon source is ash-heavy (e.g., soil-contaminated Kienyeji manure). Always check that total N is ≥ 1.5% of dry matter. Deliverable-202 calculates both.

2.2 Component roles

  • Carbon/structure base: wheat straw (preferred), barley straw, rice straw; bagasse and coffee husk as partial substitutes.
  • Nitrogen + microbial inoculum: poultry manure (layer, broiler litter, Kienyeji), horse manure, brewers' spent grain.
  • Nitrogen top-up: urea (46% N) or ammonium sulphate (21% N). Use only enough to close the gap.
  • Conditioner: agricultural gypsum (CaSO₄ · 2H₂O). It flocculates colloids so the compost does not turn greasy, supplies calcium, and buffers pH.

Analytical values are typical. Send composite samples of each new manure or straw source to a soil/feed laboratory (N, DM, ash) and re-run Deliverable-202.**

Worked example (Recipe KE-A), verified in Deliverable-202: Dry matter = 1,389.6 kg · Carbon = 549.4 kg · Nitrogen = 20.98 kg → C:N = 26.2:1, N = 1.51% DM. Remove the 10 kg of urea and C:N jumps to 33.4:1, too lean. This is why the small urea top-up matters.

Water requirement: to bring the KE-A mix (1,850 kg wet, 1,389.6 kg DM) to 72% moisture, total wet mass must be 1,389.6 / (1–0.72) ≈ 4,963 kg, so ≈ 3.1 m³ of water per tonne of straw before evaporation losses. Add 20–30% more in January–February and September–October.

Compost output: with ≈ 33% dry-matter loss through Phase I and II and 68% final moisture, 1 t of straw yields ≈ 2.9 t of spawn-ready compost. A 100 m² bed at 90 kg/m² therefore needs ≈ 3.1 t of straw, or ≈ 3.4 t with a 10% handling allowance.

3. Phase I — Outdoor / Yard Composting

Phase I is a solid-state fermentation lasting about 10–16 days. Thermophilic microbes soften the straw, release ammonia, and build the brown compost matrix. Stack centres reach 65–80 °C.

3.1 Yard design — global vs Kenyan

Element Industrial (EU/USA) Kenyan Track K1 Kenyan Track K2
Floor Concrete with aeration spigots (bunkers) Roofed concrete slab (≥ 150 mm), 1–2% fall to a leachate channel Roofed slab with aerated bunker floor
Rain protection Indoor buildings with odour scrubbers Roof mandatory. Two rainy seasons will otherwise waterlog and leach the stack Roof + side curtains
Heat/wind protection Enclosed Shade-net side curtains on the windward side; tarpaulin cover at night in the dry season Enclosed
Turning Turning machines, front loaders Teams with forks, or a tractor-mounted turner shared via a cooperative Front loader + turner
Leachate Closed recirculation Lined goody-water pit with pump-back for wetting (NEMA condition: zero discharge) Same
Aeration Bunker floor blowers (shortens Phase I to 3–6 days, reduces odour) Passive: turning only Bunker blowers

3.2 Stack geometry

  • Height 1.5–2.0 m, width 1.8–2.0 m, any length. Smaller stacks lose heat; larger ones turn anaerobic at the core.
  • In cold highland nights (Jun–Aug, Timau and Molo), lean toward 2.0 m height and cover the stack at night to keep the core above 65 °C.
  • In dry, windy months, side curtains and frequent edge wetting stop the outer 20–30 cm drying out and staying raw.

3.3 Turning and moisture schedule (standard 13-day Phase I)

Day Action Targets & checks

3.4 The squeeze test (field moisture check)

Take a handful from 30–50 cm inside the stack and squeeze firmly:

  • Water streams out: > 78%, too wet. Turn and aerate, add dry straw or bagasse, stop watering.
  • A few drops between the fingers: ≈ 70–75%, correct for Phase I.
  • Hand only damp, no drops: < 68%, too dry. Water while turning.

Calibrate your crew's squeeze test monthly with an oven-dry test: weigh 200 g, dry at 105 °C to constant weight, and calculate moisture = (wet − dry) ÷ wet × 100.

3.5 Kenyan seasonal adaptations

Season Risk Adaptation
Jan–Feb, Sep–Oct (hot/dry) Fast evaporation; dry edges; temperature overshoot > 80 °C (which sterilises the stack and stalls it) Water at each turn plus daily edge misting; side curtains; turn on schedule (do not delay); night tarp
Mar–May, Oct–Dec (rains) Waterlogging; anaerobic core; foul odour (H₂S); leachate overflow Roof and gutters; raise the slab edge; reduce added water by measured squeeze tests; extra turn if odour appears; enlarge the goody pit before the rains
Jun–Aug (cool) Slow heating; cold edges Larger stacks, night covers, pre-warmed goody water if available

4. Phase II — Pasteurisation & Conditioning

Phase II happens indoors in a controlled, forced-air environment. It must (a) kill pests and competitors, (b) convert remaining ammonia into microbial protein, and (c) leave a selective, ammonia-free substrate.

Audit correction — stage sequence. Earlier versions of this course placed conditioning before the pasteurisation peak. The standard industrial sequence is fill → level → pasteurise → condition → cool. Conditioning after the peak re-establishes the beneficial thermophilic actinomycetes and fungi (the "fire-fang" flora) and lets them clear the ammonia released during the peak.

Audit clarification — ammonia thresholds. NH₃ < 150 ppm (air in the tunnel headspace) is the conditioning milestone: it shows ammonia conversion is well under way and the compost can be held at conditioning temperature to finish. Compost may only be released for cool-down and spawning at ≤ 5–10 ppm, with no detectable ammonia smell. Spawn added to compost still holding ammonia will die or stall.

4.1 Stage-by-stage parameters

Stage Compost temp Air (supply) Duration Purpose / exit criteria
A. Fill & level Equalise to 48–51 °C Recirculation with minimal fresh air 12–24 h Uniform temperature (spread ≤ 2 °C between probes)
B. Pasteurisation peak 58–62 °C core Recirculation; steam if needed 8–12 h at temperature Kills nematodes, fly eggs/larvae, mites, competitor moulds. Do not exceed 63 °C: you kill the beneficial thermophiles and make the compost non-selective
C. Conditioning 45–50 °C (hold ≈ 48 °C) Fresh air sufficient to keep O₂ high and remove NH₃ 3–6 days Air NH₃: passes < 150 ppm milestone, then reaches ≤ 5–10 ppm; grey-white fire-fang flecks throughout; sweet, bready smell
D. Cool-down Ramp down to 24–26 °C Filtered fresh air 12–24 h Spawn-ready; release to the spawning hall

4.2 Industrial tunnel (global / Track K2)

  • Insulated concrete or panel tunnel; perforated/slatted floor (≈ 20–25% open area) over a pressurised plenum.
  • Fill depth 1.8–2.5 m at uniform density; blower capacity commonly ≈ 150–250 m³/h per tonne of compost at high static pressure, with dampers for fresh-air and recirculation mixing.
  • Temperature probes at 3–4 depths and positions; air-temperature sensors in the plenum and the return; a controller running the stage logic.

4.3 Low-cost Kenyan Phase II options (Track K1)

Option Description Capacity Key controls
Insulated bulk chamber 20–30 m³ CSEB or panel chamber, raised slatted timber/steel floor, centrifugal blower, steam from an atmospheric (non-pressurised) biomass steam generator 8–15 t per batch Fill depth 1.2–1.6 m; four probe points; log every 2 h; backup power for the blower
Room (shelf) pasteurisation Fill compost into growing shelves/bags, close the room, inject steam to bring the room and compost up; the classic US bed system One room per batch Requires excellent room insulation; temperatures are less uniform, so add probes at the top, middle, and bottom tiers; longer cycle
Hub-and-spoke tunnel Cooperative-owned tunnel serving many growers (§5) 20–60 t per batch Professional operator; growers receive spawned or Phase III compost

Steam generator safety (OSHA 2007). Any closed vessel that can build pressure is a boiler/pressure vessel that must be examined by a DOSHS Approved Person. Prefer open-vented atmospheric steam generators. Never improvise pressurised steam from sealed drums. Never block vents. Keep a trained attendant on site whenever the generator is fired. See SOP-202 §6.

4.4 Spawn-ready compost — quality release checklist

  • ☐ Air ammonia ≤ 5–10 ppm (detector tube or meter) and no ammonia smell
  • ☐ Moisture 66–70% (one or two drops on a firm squeeze)
  • ☐ pH 7.2–7.8
  • ☐ Total N 2.0–2.4% DM (lab, at least monthly)
  • ☐ Dark chocolate-brown, pliable straw with abundant grey-white fire-fang flecks
  • ☐ Sweet, bready smell; no sourness, no ammonia
  • ☐ Temperature 24–26 °C at all probe points

5. The Kenyan Hub-and-Spoke Compost Model

Europe's industry scaled because specialised yards supply many growers. Kenya can use the same model with a cooperative or SACCO-owned compost hub:

  • Hub: sited near straw (Narok, Uasin Gishu, Timau) or the poultry belt (Kiambu, Kajiado), with the NEMA licence, roofed yard, bunkers, and a Phase II tunnel run by a trained compost manager.
  • Spokes: small and mid-size growers within ≈ 3–4 hours who receive spawned compost (Phase II + spawn) or fully colonised Phase III compost in bags or blocks. They need only cropping rooms, which cuts each grower's CapEx by 40–60%.
  • Benefits: one NEMA-licensed odour/leachate site instead of dozens; consistent quality; bulk straw buying power; shared equipment.
  • Logistics: spawned compost generates heat, so transport in ventilated, shaded trucks during cool hours, ≤ 3–4 hours, with compost temperature checked on arrival (Module 3).

6. Safety in the Compost Yard

Hazard Risk Control
Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) in goody pits, leachate tanks, anaerobic stacks Fatal. Paralyses the sense of smell at high concentrations Confined-space entry permits; never enter pits alone; gas detector; forced ventilation; rescue harness. Cover pits with grates
Ammonia Eye and lung damage Work upwind; respirator with ammonia cartridge in Phase II; ventilate tunnels before entry
Organic dust & mould spores (Aspergillus, actinomycetes) "Farmer's lung" / mushroom worker's lung FFP2/N95 masks during straw breaking and turning; wet materials before handling
Heat Burns (> 70 °C core), heat stress Gloves, boots; rotate crews; drinking water
Manual handling Back injury from wet compost (≈ 1 t/m³) Team lifting; wheelbarrows; mechanisation where possible
Machinery Tractor turners, blowers Guards, lock-out when servicing, trained operators only
Zoonoses Poultry manure pathogens (Salmonella, E. coli) Gloves, handwashing, no eating in the yard; tetanus vaccination for yard crews
