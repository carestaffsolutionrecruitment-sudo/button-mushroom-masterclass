1. Agro-Climatic Siting Data

1.1 Why the Kenyan highlands suit Agaricus bisporus

A. bisporus needs two very different thermal regimes:

Stage Required temperature What the Kenyan highlands provide
Spawn run (vegetative) Compost 24–25 °C (never > 27 °C) Compost is self-heating (exothermic); room air only needs to be 20–23 °C. Highland afternoons supply this; cold nights must be blocked by insulation.
Pinning & fruiting Air 16–18 °C Mean annual temperature at 1,900–2,300 m is 15–18 °C — the fruiting target is the local daily mean. A building with enough thermal mass converts the daily swing into a near-constant mean.

This is the core engineering insight of the Kenya track: at the right altitude you do not need to manufacture cold — you only need to average out the day/night swing. In Europe and the USA the same result requires chillers; in coastal West Africa it requires either chillers or a warm-tolerant species.

Altitude rule of thumb (environmental lapse rate): air temperature falls by roughly 0.6 °C per 100 m of elevation gain. Moving a site from 1,500 m (e.g., Thika) to 2,200 m (e.g., Limuru) lowers mean temperature by about 4 °C — the difference between needing a chiller and not needing one.

1.4 Altitude effects every engineer must apply

Effect Value at ~2,200 m vs sea level Consequence
Air density ≈ 0.98 kg/m³ vs 1.20 kg/m³ (≈ 80%) A fan moving a given m³/h carries ~20% less mass of air, so ~20% less heat and moisture. Upsize fans and coolers ~20–25% over sea-level catalogue sizing.
Barometric pressure ≈ 780 hPa vs 1,013 hPa Non-pressure-compensated NDIR CO₂ sensors under-read by roughly the pressure ratio (≈ 20–23%). Use pressure-compensated meters or apply the correction: CO₂(true) ≈ reading × 1,013 / P(site).
Boiling point of water ≈ 92–93 °C Atmospheric steam is cooler than at sea level but still far above the 58–62 °C pasteurisation target; allow longer heat-up times.
Solar irradiance High (clear-sky UV and short-wave are intense near the equator) Roofs are the dominant heat-gain surface (sun almost overhead). Roof insulation and radiant barriers deliver more benefit per shilling than wall insulation.

Rainwater sizing formula:

Annual harvest (m³) = Roof area (m²) × Annual rainfall (m) × Runoff coefficient C

where C ≈ 0.8–0.9 for iron-sheet roofs. Example: a 500 m² roof at Limuru (R ≈ 1.1 m) yields 500 × 1.1 × 0.85 ≈ 468 m³/yr — about 65% of the example farm's ~720 m³/yr demand. Storage must bridge the Jun–Sep and Jan–Feb dry spells: size tanks for ≥ 60 days of demand if rainwater is the main source.

3.3 Solar pumping sizing (worked example)

Hydraulic energy (kWh/day) = 1,000 × 9.81 × Head H (m) × Daily volume V (m³) ÷ 3,600,000

Example: V = 10 m³/day (peak), total dynamic head H = 120 m (80 m pumping water level + 15 m tank stand + 25 m friction/margin):

  • E(hydraulic) = 1,000 × 9.81 × 120 × 10 ÷ 3,600,000 ≈ 3.3 kWh/day
  • Submersible pump wire-to-water efficiency ≈ 45% → 3.3 / 0.45 ≈ 7.3 kWh/day
  • Kenyan highland peak sun hours ≈ 5.0–5.5 h; array derating ≈ 0.8 → 7.3 / (5.0 × 0.8) ≈ 1.8 kWp

Specify ≈ 2 kWp of PV with a DC/AC solar pump controller and ≥ 1 day of elevated storage (10 m³ tank on a 6–10 m stand gives gravity pressure for misting and cleaning without a booster pump).

4. Energy

  • Grid tariff: Kenya Power small-commercial energy costs, all levies included, are typically KES 22–32 per kWh. Read your actual bill, which includes fuel-cost, forex and inflation adjustments.
  • Reliability: Outages and voltage dips are common in rural feeders. Anything holding live product (cold room, spawn-run fans, Phase II blower) needs backup: generator with automatic transfer switch, or a solar-plus-battery inverter sized for the critical load.
  • Biomass for steam: Use sustainably sourced firewood or briquettes (sawdust, bagasse, coffee husk briquettes are locally made). Keep purchase receipts — transport of charcoal/firewood is regulated.

1. Regional Adaptation Frameworks

A commercial button mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) facility exists to hold two very different microclimates: a warm, high-CO₂ spawn run, then a cool, fresh-air fruiting regime. How you hold them depends on your climate and capital.

1.1 Europe & USA — Industrial benchmark

  • Envelope: Insulated sandwich panels (80–120 mm PIR/PUR core, aluminium or food-grade coated steel skins) on concrete floors with integral drains. The Netherlands, Ireland, and Poland use multi-tier aluminium Dutch shelf rooms; Pennsylvania (USA) still runs many wooden bed/shelf farms with room steam pasteurisation.
  • Automation: Air Handling Units (AHUs) with cooling coils fed by closed-loop chillers, steam or hot-water heating coils, mixing boxes that blend recirculated and fresh air, positive-pressure filtered plenums, and computerised climate controllers (SCADA-type) running every stage.
  • Supply chain model: Most European growers buy Phase III (fully spawn-run) compost from specialised compost companies and only crop it. Room turnover is fast (≈ 5–6 weeks per crop) and yields are 30–35 kg/m².

1.2 West Africa — Hot-tropical track

  • Thermal management: Year-round lowland ambient temperatures of 25–33 °C make passive A. bisporus fruiting impossible.
  • Strain selection: Use warm-weather Agaricus bitorquis strains, which spawn-run hotter and fruit at about 24–28 °C, or run fully refrigerated rooms for A. bisporus.
  • Infrastructure: Earth-sheltered root-cellar rooms, double-walled shade houses, roof-thatch drip systems that keep the envelope evaporatively cooled, and night-time ventilation. Highland pockets (e.g., Jos Plateau in Nigeria, Cameroon's western highlands) can use the Kenyan passive model below.

1.3 Kenya & East Africa — Highland passive and semi-industrial track

  • The highland advantage: At 1,900–2,300 m (Limuru, Tigoni, upper Kiambu, Nyeri, Eldoret, Molo) the mean daily temperature already equals the 16–18 °C fruiting target. The engineering job is not to create cold; it is to damp the day–night swing (often 9 °C → 23 °C) into a stable mean. Thermal mass, roof insulation, and timed ventilation do that for a fraction of chiller cost. See KE-00 §1 for the site table.
  • Two build tracks:
  • Track K1 — Passive / low-energy: Compressed Stabilized Earth Block (CSEB) or machine-cut stone walls, insulated or double-thatched roofs, earth-air tubes, night-flush ventilation, evaporative curtains. CapEx ≈ KES 15,000–22,000 per m² of building.
  • Track K2 — Semi-industrial: Locally fabricated PIR/EPS sandwich-panel rooms, ducted inverter split or chiller cooling, controller-managed fresh air, Phase II tunnel. CapEx ≈ KES 45,000–80,000 per m² of building.
  • Altitude corrections: Air at 2,200 m is about 20% less dense, so fans and coolers must be upsized; NDIR CO₂ meters under-read unless pressure-compensated; water boils at ≈ 93 °C (KE-00 §1.4).
  • Cold-site caveat: At Timau or upper Molo (> 2,400 m), nights of 3–7 °C in July–August can stall the spawn run. Design those rooms with heat retention first (insulation, airtight doors) and a small thermostatic heater.

2.2 Biosecure "dirty-to-clean" layout

Material moves one way only: dirty → clean. People move clean → dirty within a day, never back.

  • Keep the compost yard ≥ 30 m from growing rooms and downwind; flies and Trichoderma spores travel from compost to crop.

2.3 Building orientation for an equatorial site

Near the equator the sun is close to overhead at noon and rises and sets almost due east and west all year.

  • Run the long axis east–west. The long walls then face north and south and receive little direct sun; only the short gable walls see low-angle morning and afternoon sun.
  • Shade east/west gables with roof overhangs, a veranda, a store room, or a planted windbreak (indigenous trees or Grevillea, kept ≥ 5 m from walls).
  • The roof is the dominant heat-gain surface. Spend insulation money on the roof first (§3.3).

3.2 Interior finish — hygiene audit correction

Audit note: earlier versions recommended bare earth floors and exposed earth walls inside grow rooms. That is withdrawn. Bare earth harbours nematodes, sciarid larvae, Trichoderma, and Mycogone, and cannot be disinfected.

  • Floors: 100 mm concrete slab with a hard trowelled screed sloped 1:100 to a trapped floor drain. Coved floor–wall joints. For floor-wetting humidification, use a shallow screed channel along the wall, not bare earth.
  • Walls: Cement–lime plaster finished with washable oil-based or epoxy paint, or PVC cladding. It must withstand chlorine and peroxide disinfectants.
  • Earth surfaces are acceptable on the outside of the clean zone (corridors, stores) and for evaporative cooling elements.

3.4 Vapour control, airtightness, and condensation

  • Fruiting rooms run at 85–95% RH. Place the vapour barrier on the warm, humid (inner) side of insulation, and seal all penetrations.
  • Fit doors with rubber gaskets. Airtightness is what lets you hold CO₂ during the spawn run and keep heat in on cold highland nights.
  • Anti-condensation: Insulated ceilings prevent cold-surface drip. Over shelves, fit sloped polythene or aluminium baffles that shed drips to the aisle. Drips on caps cause bacterial blotch.

3.5 Pest-proofing the structure

  • Termites: pre-construction soil treatment by a licensed applicator, or a physical barrier (graded stone or stainless mesh), plus treated timber.
  • Rodents: 600 mm concrete apron, sealed service entries, door sweeps.
  • Safari ants (siafu) and other ants: a water moat or sticky barrier around shelf legs in passive houses, and grease bands on posts.
  • Flies: every opening screened with ≤ 0.6 mm insect netting (sciarid and phorid adults are ≈ 2–3 mm). Fine screens cut airflow by 50–70%, so make the screened area 2–3× the free vent area.

4. Passive Climate Systems (Track K1)

4.1 Earth–air heat exchanger ("earth tube")

At 1.5–2.0 m depth, soil temperature stays near the annual mean air temperature (≈ 15–17 °C at Limuru, ≈ 16–18 °C at Nyeri and Eldoret), which is almost exactly the fruiting target.

  • Design: 20–30 m of 150–200 mm smooth-bore PVC per tube, buried 1.5–2.0 m deep, laid at a 1–2% fall to a condensate drain at the low end, with an insect-screened and filtered intake hood ≥ 1 m above ground.
  • Output: ≈ 100–200 m³/h per tube at 16–19 °C outlet temperature (verify on site). Three to five tubes per 100 m² of bed supply the base fresh air for fruiting through an inline fan.
  • Hygiene: fit a removable inlet filter and clean the tube annually with a pull-through brush or a chlorine flush. Condensate pooling breeds bacteria, so the drain must work.

4.2 Night-flush ventilation

Open low intakes and high gable/ridge outlets during the coolest hours (≈ 22:00–07:00, adjusted to your logger data) to purge the heat stored in the walls and the CO₂. Seal up before the morning temperature rise. The stack effect (warm air rising out of high vents) drives the flow with no fans when the vertical separation between inlet and outlet is ≥ 2.5 m. Full operating schedule: SOP-101.

4.3 Evaporative cooling

  • Hessian/coir curtains or cellulose pads over intakes, kept wet with a gravity drip line from the elevated tank.
  • Effectiveness depends on the wet-bulb depression: large in January–February and September–October (dry air, 6–10 °C of cooling possible), small in the rainy seasons (1–3 °C). Plan evaporative cooling for the hot dry seasons and do not rely on it in March–May.
  • Hessian grows mould. Rotate two sets weekly, sun-dry, and replace monthly.

4.4 Heat retention and supplementary heat (cold highland sites)

  • The spawn-run compost generates its own heat. Insulate and close the room, and room air at 20–23 °C keeps the compost at 24–25 °C.
  • If night room air drops below ≈ 19 °C and the compost core falls below 22 °C: add a thermostatically controlled oil-filled electric heater (1.5–2 kW per 100 m² of bed is a typical starting point), or hot-water pipes from a solar water heater with a backup element. Never use open charcoal jikos or paraffin burners. They produce carbon monoxide (lethal to workers) and ethylene/combustion gases that damage the crop.

5.2 Fan and fresh-air sizing (worked example, Limuru altitude)

Room: 10 m × 7 m × 3.5 m = 245 m³, 100 m² of shelf area.

  3. Filter, screen, and duct losses: select a fan rated for ≈ 1,850–2,000 m³/h at 150–250 Pa static pressure, with a variable-speed controller so the same fan can run at 20–30% during spawn run.

5.3 Cooling load (Track K2, Nairobi peri-urban example)

Sensible load components for a 100 m² bed room during fruiting: envelope gain (≈ 1–2 kW with insulated panels), fresh-air load (1,840 m³/h of 26 °C air cooled to 17 °C at 0.98 kg/m³: ≈ 0.51 kg/s × 1.006 × 9 ≈ 4.6 kW), and crop respiration plus compost heat (≈ 1.5–3 kW in the first flush). Design ≈ 8–10 kW (≈ 2.5–3 TR) of cooling per 100 m² of bed for a lowland-edge site, versus ≈ 0–3 kW for a well-built passive room at Limuru. Module 2: Substrate Engineering — Phase I & Phase II Composting

Learning outcomes. You will be able to (1) formulate a compost recipe from Kenyan raw materials using C:N, nitrogen, and moisture targets, (2) run an outdoor Phase I yard through Kenyan wet and dry seasons, (3) run Phase II pasteurisation and conditioning in an industrial tunnel or a low-cost chamber, and (4) judge whether compost is ready to spawn.

Linked documents: SOP-201 · SOP-202 · Deliverable-202 · KE-00 §2 (sourcing), §3 (water), §5 (NEMA)

1. Why Agaricus Needs Selective Compost

Unlike wood-rotting mushrooms (oyster, shiitake), Agaricus bisporus is a secondary decomposer. It grows on a substrate that thermophilic bacteria, actinomycetes, and fungi have already partly digested. Composting has two jobs:

  1. Nutrition: turn straw cellulose and hemicellulose plus manure nitrogen into a lignin–humus–protein complex that Agaricus can use and most competitors cannot.
  2. Selectivity: remove free ammonia and simple sugars and kill pests, so that when spawn is added, Agaricus is the only organism able to colonise quickly.

Poor compost cannot be rescued later in the crop. Most low yields in Kenya trace back to Phase I and Phase II.

2.2 Component roles

  • Carbon/structure base: wheat straw (preferred), barley straw, rice straw; bagasse and coffee husk as partial substitutes.
  • Nitrogen + microbial inoculum: poultry manure (layer, broiler litter, Kienyeji), horse manure, brewers' spent grain.
  • Nitrogen top-up: urea (46% N) or ammonium sulphate (21% N). Use only enough to close the gap.
  • Conditioner: agricultural gypsum (CaSO₄ · 2H₂O). It flocculates colloids so the compost does not turn greasy, supplies calcium, and buffers pH.

Worked example (Recipe KE-A), verified in Deliverable-202: Dry matter = 1,389.6 kg · Carbon = 549.4 kg · Nitrogen = 20.98 kg → C:N = 26.2:1, N = 1.51% DM. Remove the 10 kg of urea and C:N jumps to 33.4:1, too lean. This is why the small urea top-up matters.

Phase I is a solid-state fermentation lasting about 10–16 days. Thermophilic microbes soften the straw, release ammonia, and build the brown compost matrix. Stack centres reach 65–80 °C.

3.4 The squeeze test (field moisture check)

Take a handful from 30–50 cm inside the stack and squeeze firmly:

  • Water streams out: > 78%, too wet. Turn and aerate, add dry straw or bagasse, stop watering.
  • A few drops between the fingers: ≈ 70–75%, correct for Phase I.
  • Hand only damp, no drops: < 68%, too dry. Water while turning.

Calibrate your crew's squeeze test monthly with an oven-dry test: weigh 200 g, dry at 105 °C to constant weight, and calculate moisture = (wet − dry) ÷ wet × 100.

4. Phase II — Pasteurisation & Conditioning

Phase II happens indoors in a controlled, forced-air environment. It must (a) kill pests and competitors, (b) convert remaining ammonia into microbial protein, and (c) leave a selective, ammonia-free substrate.

Audit correction — stage sequence. Earlier versions of this course placed conditioning before the pasteurisation peak. The standard industrial sequence is fill → level → pasteurise → condition → cool. Conditioning after the peak re-establishes the beneficial thermophilic actinomycetes and fungi (the "fire-fang" flora) and lets them clear the ammonia released during the peak.

Audit clarification — ammonia thresholds. NH₃ < 150 ppm (air in the tunnel headspace) is the conditioning milestone: it shows ammonia conversion is well under way and the compost can be held at conditioning temperature to finish. Compost may only be released for cool-down and spawning at ≤ 5–10 ppm, with no detectable ammonia smell. Spawn added to compost still holding ammonia will die or stall.

  • Temperature probes at 3–4 depths and positions; air-temperature sensors in the plenum and the return; a controller running the stage logic.

5. The Kenyan Hub-and-Spoke Compost Model

Europe's industry scaled because specialised yards supply many growers. Kenya can use the same model with a cooperative or SACCO-owned compost hub:

  • Hub: sited near straw (Narok, Uasin Gishu, Timau) or the poultry belt (Kiambu, Kajiado), with the NEMA licence, roofed yard, bunkers, and a Phase II tunnel run by a trained compost manager.
  • Spokes: small and mid-size growers within ≈ 3–4 hours who receive spawned compost (Phase II + spawn) or fully colonised Phase III compost in bags or blocks. They need only cropping rooms, which cuts each grower's CapEx by 40–60%.
  • Benefits: one NEMA-licensed odour/leachate site instead of dozens; consistent quality; bulk straw buying power; shared equipment.
  • Logistics: spawned compost generates heat, so transport in ventilated, shaded trucks during cool hours, ≤ 3–4 hours, with compost temperature checked on arrival (Module 3).

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP defines the step-by-step management of Phase I outdoor/yard composting, from raw-material receiving to the hand-over to Phase II. It covers recipe execution, pre-wetting, stack building, turning, moisture and temperature control, seasonal adaptations, leachate management, and yard safety.

2. Responsibilities

Role Responsibility
Compost Yard Manager Recipe (Deliverable-202), batch sheet sign-off, release to Phase II
Yard Supervisor Daily temperature/moisture checks; directs turning crews
Yard Crew Pre-wetting, stacking, turning, goody-pit management
Safety Officer / Manager Confined-space permits; PPE compliance

3. PPE & Safety (mandatory)

  • Gumboots, heavy gloves, overalls; FFP2/N95 masks while breaking dry straw and turning.
  • Goody-water and leachate pits are confined spaces. Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) can kill in minutes. No entry without a permit, gas test, forced ventilation, a standby person, and a rescue harness. Keep pits covered with grates.
  • Stack cores reach 70–80 °C: risk of burns when opening.
  • Tractor/turner work: trained operator only; no one within 5 m of moving machinery; lock-out for maintenance.
  • Drinking water and shade for crews in hot months; rotate heavy tasks.
  • Tetanus vaccination for yard crews; wash hands before eating (poultry manure carries Salmonella and E. coli).

4. Materials & Equipment

  • Raw materials per Deliverable-202 batch sheet (straw, manure, bagasse/husk, urea, gypsum)
  • Platform or weighbridge scale (or bale counts × verified average bale weight)
  • Water supply ≥ 3–4 m³ per tonne of straw, plus a goody-water recirculation pump
  • Probe thermometer, 1 m stainless (or a 60 cm probe used from both sides)
  • Hay forks, wheelbarrows; tractor/front loader and turner where available
  • Tarpaulins/shade net; moisture oven or microwave + kitchen scale for calibration

Local workarounds: * No turning machine: a crew of 6–8 with forks can turn ≈ 10–15 t per day. Work in a "rolling" pattern: rebuild the stack 2 m along from its old position, throwing the outside to the inside. * No weighbridge: weigh 10 random bales on a hanging scale and use the average × bale count; weigh manure by the bag. * No lab moisture meter: oven-dry test (§7.2) monthly to calibrate the crew's squeeze test.

5. Procedure

5.1 Receiving raw materials (Day −10 to −7)

  1. Check straw: golden, dry, long-stem, free of grey/black mould and soil. Reject rained-on bales.
  2. Check manure: no plastics, feathers, stones; note the supplier and batch; take a 1 kg composite sample for lab N/DM analysis (new suppliers and monthly thereafter).
  3. Check gypsum with a drop of vinegar: no fizz (fizzing indicates limestone, which is the wrong material).
  4. Record weights on the Deliverable-202 batch sheet.

5.2 Pre-wetting (Day −7 to −1)

  1. Break bales open; spread straw in a 1.0–1.5 m layer on the slab.
  2. Wet thoroughly with sprinklers or hoses; recirculate goody water from the pit.
  3. Turn the straw heap every 2 days, re-wetting dry patches. Kenyan dry Rift straw may need the full 5–7 days; barley straw needs fewer.
  4. From Day −3, mix in ≈ ⅓ of the manure to start microbial activity (optional, recommended for very dry straw).
  5. Target at Day 0: straw dark, soft, fully wetted to the centre of the stems.

5.3 Stack building (Day 0)

  1. Build alternating layers: wetted straw (≈ 30 cm) → manure → bagasse/husk (if used) → urea spread evenly by hand broadcast (never in lumps: hotspots of free ammonia).
  2. Stack dimensions: 1.5–2.0 m high, 1.8–2.0 m wide, sides vertical, top flat or slightly domed.
  3. Add water while building until the squeeze test gives a few drops (70–75%).
  4. Insert temperature check points at 50 cm depth every 3 m along the stack.
  5. Record on the batch sheet: time, weights, water volume, and weather.

5.4 Turning schedule

Day Action Record

7 Second turn: broadcast gypsum (3–5% of dry mass) evenly; water as needed As above + gypsum weight
10 Third turn As above

Daily (non-turn days): core temperature at 3–5 points; visual check of edges; squeeze test at 2 points.

5.5 Goody water and leachate

  • All runoff flows into the lined goody pit; pump it back for pre-wetting and turning.
  • Keep the pit ≥ 30 cm below its overflow before each rainy season. Never discharge to drains, streams, or land outside the approved area (NEMA licence condition).

5.6 End-of-Phase I release criteria (Day 13)

  • ☐ Dark brown, pliable straw that breaks when twisted
  • ☐ Moisture 70–74% (squeeze: a few drops)
  • ☐ Strong but not putrid ammonia smell; no rotten-egg (H₂S) smell
  • ☐ pH ≈ 8.0–8.5
  • ☐ Uniform; no white, dry "fire-fanged" bands of raw straw

Standard Operating Procedure: Phase II Tunnel Pasteurization & Ammonia Clearance (SOP-202)

  • Document ID: SOP-202
  • Version: 2.0
  • Target Region: Global indoor bulk tunnels, with a Kenyan low-cost chamber variant
  • Applies To: Facility Operators, Climate Technicians, Boiler/Steam Attendants
  • Linked Documents: Module 2 §4 · SOP-201 · Deliverable-101

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP covers loading, levelling, pasteurisation, conditioning (ammonia clearance), and cool-down of Phase I compost in (a) an industrial tunnel or (b) a low-cost insulated chamber heated by an atmospheric biomass steam generator. The output is selective, ammonia-free compost ready for spawning.

Sequence: fill → level → pasteurise → condition → cool. (Version 1.0 listed conditioning before the peak. Corrected in 2.0.)

2. Responsibilities

Role Responsibility
Phase II Operator Runs the cycle; logs probe readings every 2 h (manual) or verifies controller logs
Steam Attendant Operates the steam generator; never leaves it unattended while fired
Compost Manager Approves release to spawning against the release checklist

3. Safety

  • Ammonia: respirator with an ammonia (K-type) cartridge when entering a tunnel during conditioning; ventilate before entry.
  • Steam and hot compost: heat-resistant gloves; no entry during the pasteurisation peak.
  • Steam generator (OSHA 2007): any vessel able to hold pressure must be examined by a DOSHS Approved Person and kept within its certificate. Use open-vented atmospheric steam generators for low-cost setups. Never improvise with sealed drums or blocked vents. Keep a water-level sight glass, a free vent, and a trained attendant.
  • Confined space: tunnels and plenums: permit, gas test (NH₃, O₂), standby person.
  • Electrical: blower motors earthed and RCD-protected; backup generator transfer switch tested monthly.

4. Equipment

Item Industrial Kenyan low-cost variant
Vessel Insulated tunnel with slatted/spigot floor Insulated CSEB or panel chamber (20–30 m³), raised slatted timber/steel floor over a 30–50 cm plenum
Air High-pressure fan, fresh/recirculation dampers Centrifugal blower (≈ 150–250 m³/h per tonne of compost) with a manual slide damper for fresh air
Heat Steam injection or hot-water coil Atmospheric biomass steam generator (firewood/briquettes) piped into the plenum
Sensors 4–8 compost probes + air sensors on a controller 4 digital probe thermometers at fixed points (front/back × top/middle) read every 2 h
Ammonia Electrochemical sensor Detector tubes (or a handheld meter) and the trained-nose check

5. Procedure

5.1 Loading (Hour 0)

  1. Pre-clean and disinfect the tunnel/chamber after the previous batch; check the floor slots are clear.
  2. Load Phase I compost uniformly (industrial 1.8–2.5 m depth; low-cost chamber 1.2–1.6 m). Do not compact or walk on the compost. Fill loosely and level.
  3. Insert probes at the agreed fixed points (≥ 4).
  4. Close doors; start recirculation at a low fresh-air setting.

5.2 Stage A — Levelling (≈ 12–24 h)

  • Recirculate to equalise the mass at 48–51 °C; spread between probes ≤ 2 °C.
  • Compost's own activity provides most of the heat; add steam only to lift cold spots.

5.3 Stage B — Pasteurisation peak (8–12 h)

  • Raise the compost core to 58–62 °C at all probe points (steam into the plenum + recirculation).
  • Hold for 8–12 h after the coldest probe reaches 58 °C.
  • Never exceed 63 °C in the compost. That kills the beneficial thermophiles needed for conditioning.
  • Air temperature in the plenum may run 2–5 °C below compost; control on compost probes.

5.4 Stage C — Conditioning (≈ 3–6 days)

  1. Lower to 45–50 °C (hold ≈ 48 °C) by opening fresh air gradually.
  2. Supply enough fresh air to keep compost aerobic: sweet smell, no sourness.
  3. Measure air NH₃ in the return/headspace every 12 h: * Milestone: NH₃ < 150 ppm means conversion is well under way. Continue holding at conditioning temperature. * Release threshold: NH₃ ≤ 5–10 ppm, and no ammonia smell at the compost surface.
  4. Look for abundant grey-white fire-fang (actinomycete) flecks throughout.

5.5 Stage D — Cool-down (≈ 12–24 h)

  • Increase filtered fresh air to bring the compost to 24–26 °C at all probes.
  • At highland sites, night air cools fastest and cheapest. Schedule the cool-down overnight.
  • Keep the compost covered/closed until spawning to prevent fly entry.

6. Release Checklist (Compost Manager signs)

  • ☐ Peak held at 58–62 °C for ≥ 8 h at all probes (log attached)
  • ☐ Conditioning milestone < 150 ppm recorded (date/time)
  • ☐ Release reading ≤ 5–10 ppm NH₃; no smell
  • ☐ Moisture 66–70%; pH 7.2–7.8
  • ☐ Colour dark brown; fire-fang flecks present; sweet, bready smell
  • ☐ All probes 24–26 °C

7. Kenyan Operational Notes

  • Power cuts: a blower stop during the peak or conditioning creates anaerobic zones and hot spots within an hour. Generator start is mandatory; log every outage with duration.
  • Altitude: atmospheric steam is ≈ 92–93 °C at 2,000–2,300 m. Heat-up is slower than sea-level manuals suggest; start steam earlier.
  • Biomass fuel: keep ≥ 1.5 batches of dry fuel in stock (wet firewood in the rains produces little steam); log fuel use per batch to track efficiency.
  • Ammonia tubes are costly: use them at the milestone and at release; in between, use trained-nose checks and pH strips on condensate.

Module 3: Aseptic Spawning, Spawn Run, & Casing Science

Learning outcomes. You will be able to (1) select and source spawn and protect it through a Kenyan road cold chain, (2) spawn and fill compost cleanly at the correct rate, (3) hold the spawn run inside its narrow thermal window in highland conditions, and (4) formulate, buffer, pasteurise, and apply a casing layer from locally available materials.

Linked documents: SOP-301 · SOP-302 · Deliverable-303 · Deliverable-101 · KE-00 §2.2–2.3

1. Strain Selection

Strain type Characteristics Market fit (Kenya) Market fit (global)
Smooth white hybrid (U1-derived commercial lines) High yield, white smooth cap, bruises visibly Supermarket punnets (Naivas, Quickmart, Carrefour), hotels; the dominant Kenyan demand EU/USA fresh market staple
Off-white / cream Sturdier cap, slightly better shelf life, more tolerant of handling Open-air wholesale markets, longer-haul routes (Eldoret → Kisumu/Kampala) Processing and canning
Brown (chestnut / cremini → portobello) Lower yield (≈ 10–20% less), better shelf life, richer flavour Premium hotels, restaurants, upmarket retail. Niche but higher price Growing specialty segment

Strain selection rules: 1. Trial a new strain on one room before switching the whole farm. 2. Keep a strain log with yields per flush and disease incidence (Deliverable-404). 3. Ask suppliers for the strain's temperature profile. Some white hybrids tolerate the 18–19 °C fruiting temperatures that Kenyan passive rooms reach on warm afternoons better than others.

2. Spawn Sourcing and the Kenyan Cold Chain

2.1 Supply options

  • Local spawn laboratories (Juja/Nairobi/Nakuru area and other university or private labs): shortest cold chain, local payment, faster reorders. Audit the lab before your first order: clean-room practice, sterilisation logs, mother-culture renewal, and a written purity guarantee.
  • Imported spawn (European or South African spawn houses): consistent genetics. It needs a KEPHIS plant import permit and phytosanitary certificate, refrigerated air freight, and clearance at JKIA within hours.

2.2 Spawn biology that drives logistics

Grain spawn is alive and respiring. Above ≈ 8 °C it generates heat; packed tightly in a warm vehicle, the core of a spawn box can rise past 30 °C and kill or weaken the mycelium and favour bacteria. Cold, ventilated, and not over-stacked is the rule.

2.3 Road transport protocol (lab → farm)

Step Requirement Kenyan field practice
Packaging Spawn bags in rigid crates/boxes, not loose Plastic crates protect bags from vibration damage on murram roads
Cooling Hold 2–8 °C (ideal 2–4 °C), never frozen Quality insulated cooler boxes with frozen gel packs wrapped in cardboard or newspaper (direct contact freezes grain). Ratio ≈ 1 kg of gel pack per 3–4 kg of spawn for trips ≤ 8 h
Monitoring Logged temperature A cheap USB/Bluetooth logger in every consignment; reject or quarantine if > 15 °C for > 4 h
Timing Travel in cool hours Depart at dawn; avoid midday traffic on the Nairobi bypasses/Thika Road; never leave coolers in a parked car in the sun
Vehicle Shaded, ventilated Inside the cab or a covered pickup with a tarp shade and airflow. Never on a matatu roof rack. For long hauls (Eldoret, Nyeri, Timau), use refrigerated courier or overnight bus parcel services with gel-pack coolers rated ≥ 18 h
Hand-over Immediate refrigeration Farm fridge or cold room at 2–4 °C on arrival; record arrival temperature on the delivery note

2.4 Spawn receiving inspection

  • ☐ Uniform, bright white mycelium through every grain; no dry or uncolonised patches
  • ☐ No green, black, yellow, or pink spots (Trichoderma, Penicillium, Neurospora, other moulds)
  • ☐ No sour, fruity, or rotten smell and no wet or greasy grains (bacterial "wet spot", Bacillus)
  • ☐ Not over-aged: minimal clumping, no pins or primordia, no yellow exudate
  • ☐ Bag integrity intact; label shows strain, lot number, and production date
  • ☐ Arrival temperature recorded ≤ 8 °C

Reject or quarantine any failing bag. Contaminated spawn infects the entire batch of compost it is mixed into.

2.5 Storage

2–4 °C, stacked with air gaps, used within the supplier's stated shelf life (typically 4–8 weeks). Bring spawn out 6–12 h before use without opening the bags, so condensation forms on the outside of the bag rather than on the grain.

3. Spawning & Filling

3.1 Inoculation rate

  • 0.5% – 1.0% of the wet compost weight. Most commercial farms use ≈ 0.6–0.8%.
  • Example: 9,000 kg of compost × 0.7% = 63 kg of spawn.
  • Higher rates (towards 1.0%) shorten the spawn run and help colonisation outrun competitors. That is worth paying for in the rainy seasons, when contamination pressure is high, or when compost quality is uncertain.

  • Clean before dirty: spawning happens in a room cleaned and disinfected after the previous use.
  • Staff in clean overalls, hair covered, hands washed and sanitised, boots through a footbath.
  • All tools, conveyors, and forks disinfected; no compost dust tracked in from the yard.
  • Fill, close, and record each bag or tray batch with room, date, and lot number.

4. Spawn Run (Colonisation)

4.1 Environmental window

Parameter Target Why
Compost core temperature 24–25 °C Fastest healthy growth
Absolute ceiling 27 °C; alarm at 26.5 °C Above ≈ 27 °C the mycelium is damaged and competitors (Trichoderma, thermophilic moulds) gain the advantage. Above ≈ 30 °C the damage is severe and irreversible
Floor ≥ 22 °C Below this the run slows sharply and competitors catch up
Room air 20–23 °C The compost's own heat supplies the difference
RH 90–95% Prevents surface drying
CO₂ 5,000–10,000 ppm Promotes vegetative growth and suppresses premature pinning
Duration 14–18 days to full colonisation Monitor visually: compost should turn evenly grey-white

4.2 Highland thermal management

Compost heat output peaks around days 4–10 of the spawn run. On a Kenyan highland farm that means two opposite risks in the same room:

  • Warm days (Jan–Feb, Sep–Oct) + peak compost heat → overheating. Responses, in order: open the bag tops slightly, increase recirculation, run night-time fresh air (cold highland night air is your free chiller), space bags further apart (≥ 5 cm), reduce bag size for the next batch (10–12 kg instead of 18–20 kg).
  • Cold nights (Jun–Aug, high sites) → stalling. Responses: close vents fully at night, check door seals, insulate, and add thermostatic electric heating (Module 1 §4.4). Never use charcoal or paraffin heaters.

Log the compost core temperature at least twice daily (06:00–08:00 and 15:00–17:00, the coldest and hottest points of the Kenyan highland day) on Deliverable-303.

4.3 Readiness for casing

  • ☐ Compost fully colonised; no uncolonised dark pockets
  • ☐ Sweet mushroom smell; no ammonia or sourness
  • ☐ No green, olive, or pink patches (if found, see SOP-402)
  • ☐ Surface levelled and lightly firmed

5. Casing Layer Science

5.1 What casing does

A. bisporus will not fruit on compost alone. The casing layer is a nutrient-poor, water-holding, well-aerated cover that: 1. Holds the water reservoir that fruit bodies draw on (a mushroom is ~90% water). 2. Creates the CO₂ gradient (high at the compost, low at the surface) that triggers pinning. 3. Hosts beneficial bacteria (Pseudomonas putida and others) that consume the mycelium's volatile inhibitors and help induce fruiting.

5.2 Casing property targets

Property Target
pH 7.2 – 7.5 (buffered with agricultural limestone, CaCO₃)
Water-holding capacity High: material should hold ≥ 2–3× its dry weight in water
Structure Crumb, open; must not seal or crust when watered
EC (salts) < 1.0 mS/cm (1:5 water extract)
Nutrients Low (casing must not feed competitors)
Free of pathogens Pasteurised: no Mycogone, Verticillium, nematodes, fly larvae

5.3 Kenyan casing formulations

Formula Composition (by volume) Lime requirement (start point, confirm by jar test) Notes
CS-KE1 "Coir–Loam" 50% washed/buffered coco peat + 50% red volcanic subsoil (40–80 cm depth, sieved ≤ 10 mm) ≈ 15–25 kg CaCO₃ per m³ Best all-round local option. Coir adds water holding; subsoil adds structure and buffering
CS-KE2 "Sandy-Loam Mix" 60% sandy loam + 25% coco peat + 15% weathered SMC ≈ 10–20 kg/m³ Where good loam is available (e.g., parts of Nakuru/Naivasha); check salt levels of the SMC
CS-KE3 "Peat–Coir Premium" 60% imported sphagnum peat + 40% buffered coco peat ≈ 25–40 kg/m³ (peat is very acidic, pH 3.5–4.5) Highest consistency; for premium or export crops
CS-GL "Global Standard" Black peat / sphagnum peat + sugar-beet lime or limestone Per supplier EU/USA standard; often bought ready-made

Do not use black cotton soil (it seals and cracks), unpasteurised topsoil (Mycogone wet bubble and nematodes), or forest/wetland material extracted from protected areas (illegal under KFS/EMCA rules; see KE-00 §2.2).

5.4 pH buffering — the jar test (lime titration)

The lime requirement of Kenyan subsoils varies widely (red Kiambu soils at pH 4.5–5.5 need far more than Rift Valley sandy loams at pH 6.5–7.5). Never guess:

  1. Prepare five 1-litre samples of the moist casing mix.
  2. Add limestone at 0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 g per litre (= 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 kg/m³). Mix thoroughly.
  3. Moisten, cover, and leave 48–72 h (limestone reacts slowly).
  4. Measure pH with a calibrated meter (1 part casing : 5 parts distilled water, stir, settle 30 min).
  5. Choose the lowest rate that gives pH 7.2–7.5, then add ≈ 10% as a reserve: watering with acidic rainwater and acid by-products of the mycelium lower pH during cropping.
  6. High-alkalinity borehole water (KE-00 §3.4) pushes pH up over the crop, so use the lower end of the range.

5.5 Pasteurisation

  • Steam: 60–65 °C throughout the mass for 4–6 hours, then cool. In the Kenyan K1 track, use the Phase II chamber between compost batches or a dedicated small steam box.
  • Solarisation is not a substitute. Black-polythene solar heating rarely holds 60 °C throughout the mass for long enough and leaves cold pockets.
  • Store pasteurised casing covered in a clean area; use within 3–5 days.

5.6 Application

  1. Bring casing moisture to near field capacity (squeeze: holds shape, glistens, one or two drops).
  2. Apply a uniform 3–5 cm layer (thinner on bags with less compost, thicker on deep trays). Uneven depth gives uneven pinning.
  3. Water in lightly over 2–3 days, building to field capacity, without letting water run through into the compost.
  4. Case run: 7–10 days at 22–24 °C air (compost 23–25 °C), RH 90–95%, CO₂ 3,000–5,000 ppm, until mycelium shows at the casing surface across ~70% of the area.
  5. Ruffling (scratching): around day 5–7 of the case run, rake the top 2–3 cm to redistribute the mycelium. This gives more uniform, denser pinning. Clean the rakes between rooms.
  6. Advanced (industrial): CACing. Mixing ≈ 0.5–1% colonised compost into the casing ("compost added at casing") speeds the case run by 2–3 days and evens out pinning. Only do this with clean, fully colonised compost.

Next step: pinning induction (Module 4, SOP-401).

Standard Operating Procedure: Aseptic Spawning, Bag/Tray Filling, & Temperature Control (SOP-301)

  • Document ID: SOP-301
  • Version: 2.0
  • Target Region: Global spawning and incubation zones, with a Kenyan cold-chain and passive-room emphasis
  • Applies To: Inoculation Technicians, Growing-Room Operators, Stores/Receiving Staff
  • Linked Documents: Module 3 · Deliverable-303 · Deliverable-101 · SOP-202 (release checklist)

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP covers spawn receiving and storage (including road cold-chain verification), preparation of the spawning area, spawn mixing at the correct rate, filling of bags/trays/shelves, and temperature/CO₂ control through the spawn run until the compost is ready for casing.

2. Responsibilities

Role Responsibility
Stores/Receiving Spawn receiving inspection; cold storage; stock rotation (FIFO)
Inoculation Lead Area sanitation; spawn rate calculation; supervises mixing and filling
Room Operator Twice-daily spawn-run readings on Deliverable-303; corrective actions
Farm Manager Reviews Deliverable-303 daily; approves casing

3. Hygiene & Safety

  • Clean overalls (clean-zone colour), hair cover, washed and sanitised hands, boots through the footbath. No staff who worked in the compost yard that day.
  • Disinfectants: use food-safe products (e.g., chlorine at the label rate, hydrogen peroxide/peracetic acid). Wear gloves and eye protection when mixing concentrates; never mix chlorine with acids or ammonia.
  • Manual handling: compost bags of 15–20 kg; team lifting; no bag > 20 kg for a single worker.

4. Procedure

4.1 Spawn receiving (each delivery)

  1. Read the cold-chain logger or probe the spawn on arrival. Accept if ≤ 8 °C. Quarantine if > 15 °C for > 4 h in transit (check the logger trace) and contact the supplier.
  2. Inspect each bag (Module 3 §2.4): uniform white, no coloured spots, no sour or fruity smell, no wet grain, not over-aged, labels complete.
  3. Record supplier, strain, lot, production date, quantity, and arrival temperature.
  4. Store immediately at 2–4 °C, spaced for airflow, FIFO.

Local workaround — no cold room: a dedicated domestic fridge (not shared with food) set to 2–4 °C, with a thermometer. Never store spawn in the freezer compartment.

4.2 Spawning-area preparation (day before)

  1. Remove all debris; wash floors, walls, mixing surfaces, conveyors, forks, scoops, and scales with detergent, then disinfect.
  2. Close and screen the room; run filtered air if available.
  3. Take spawn out of cold storage 6–12 h before use, unopened.

4.3 Spawn rate calculation

Spawn (kg) = Compost wet weight (kg) × Spawn rate

  • Standard rate 0.6–0.8% (permitted range 0.5–1.0%).
  • Use 0.8–1.0% in the rainy seasons, with compost of uncertain quality, or with high fly pressure.
  • Example: 9,000 kg × 0.7% = 63 kg.
  • Record the calculation on the batch record.

4.4 Mixing and filling

  1. Confirm the compost release certificate (SOP-202 §6): NH₃ ≤ 5-10 ppm, 24–26 °C.
  2. Manual (Track K1): spread compost on the disinfected floor or clean sheet in a 20–30 cm layer; break spawn bags into a clean bucket, separating clumped grains by hand (gloved); broadcast evenly in 2–3 passes, mixing with clean forks after each pass. Mechanical (Track K2 / global): dose spawn onto the compost conveyor with a calibrated spawn doser; check the dose by catching 1 minute of output.
  3. Fill bags to the target weight (e.g., 15 kg ± 0.5 kg) or trays/shelves to 85–100 kg/m²; firm gently and level the surface. Do not compress hard: mycelium needs air in the compost.
  4. Fold bag tops loosely, or cover trays/shelves with clean paper or perforated polythene to hold moisture and CO₂.
  5. Label every bag/tray batch: room, date, compost batch, spawn lot.
  6. Spawning finished → clean the area immediately.

4.5 Spawn run monitoring (Days 1 – ≈ 16)

Record on Deliverable-303 twice daily: 06:00–08:00 and 15:00–17:00.

Parameter Target Action threshold
Compost core temperature 24–25 °C ≥ 26.5 °C → cooling actions (below); < 22 °C → warming actions
Absolute maximum 27 °C Emergency cooling; log as a deviation
Room air 20–23 °C Adjust to steer compost temperature
RH 90–95% < 88% → mist walls/floor
CO₂ 5,000–10,000 ppm > 12,000 ppm with compost > 26 °C → introduce cool fresh air

Probe placement: insert the probe into the centre of 3 bags per room (top tier, middle tier, bottom tier — the top tier is usually warmest) and the same 3 bags every day.

Cooling actions, in order (compost ≥ 26.5 °C): 1. Increase recirculation. 2. Introduce outside air if it is cooler than the room (night or early morning in the highlands). 3. Open bag tops slightly / lift tray covers. 4. Space bags further apart (≥ 5 cm gaps). 5. Track K2: lower the room air setpoint by 2 °C. 6. Next batch: smaller bags (10–12 kg), lower fill density, or an earlier spawn run before the hot season.

Warming actions (compost < 22 °C, typically Jun–Aug at high sites): close vents and doors fully; check seals; thermostatic electric heater (never combustion heaters); cover bags/trays.

4.6 Ready for casing (≈ Day 14–18)

  • ☐ Full, even grey-white colonisation; no dark uncolonised pockets
  • ☐ Pleasant mushroom smell
  • ☐ No contamination (green, olive, pink, yellow patches; see SOP-402)
  • ☐ Manager sign-off on Deliverable-303

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP covers sourcing, formulation, pH buffering (lime jar test), pasteurisation, moisture adjustment, application, case-run management, and ruffling of the casing layer for Agaricus bisporus.

  • Steam pasteurisation: follow SOP-202 §3 steam-generator rules.

Material Specification

5. Procedure

5.1 Formulation (volume basis)

Code Mix Starting lime rate (confirm by jar test)
CS-KE1 50% buffered coir + 50% red subsoil 15–25 kg/m³
CS-KE2 60% sandy loam + 25% coir + 15% weathered SMC 10–20 kg/m³

Quantity: bed area (m²) × depth (m) × 1.1 (settling allowance). Example: 100 m² × 0.045 m × 1.1 ≈ 5.0 m³ (budget 4.5 m³ of settled casing in Deliverable-505).

5.2 Lime jar test (every new material lot)

  1. Five 1-litre moist samples; lime at 0 / 10 / 20 / 30 / 40 g per litre (= kg/m³).
  2. Mix, moisten, cover; wait 48–72 h.
  3. pH in a 1:5 casing:distilled water suspension, after 30 min settling, with a calibrated meter (calibrate at pH 7 and 10 on the day of use).
  4. Select the lowest rate giving pH 7.2–7.5; add a ≈ 10% reserve.
  5. Using high-bicarbonate borehole water for watering? Use the lower end of the range.

Workaround if no pH meter: universal indicator strips are acceptable for screening but must be confirmed with a meter (borrowed, or at a county/university soil lab) for each new material.

5.3 Mixing

  1. Mix dry or semi-moist components on a clean, disinfected slab.
  2. Add lime evenly in three portions, turning after each.
  3. Add water gradually to near field capacity (squeeze: holds shape, glistens, 1–2 drops).

5.4 Pasteurisation

  1. Load the casing loosely (≤ 60 cm deep) into the Phase II chamber between compost batches, or into a dedicated steam box with a perforated floor.
  2. Steam until all probe points reach 60–65 °C; hold 4–6 h.
  3. Do not exceed 70 °C for long (that destroys beneficial bacteria and can release manganese/ammonium from soil components).
  4. Cool with filtered air; cover; store in the clean zone; use within 3–5 days.

5.5 Application (on fully colonised compost)

  1. Level the compost surface; remove any contaminated patches first.
  2. Apply casing to a uniform 3–5 cm; check depth with a marked stick at 5 points per bag-row/shelf.
  3. Water in over 2–3 days with a fine rose (≈ 1–2 L/m² per application) until the casing reaches field capacity. Do not let water pass into the compost.

5.6 Case run (≈ 7–10 days)

  • Air 22–24 °C; compost 23–25 °C; RH 90–95%; CO₂ 3,000–5,000 ppm.
  • Ruffling: around Day 5–7, when mycelium has grown about two-thirds of the way through the casing, rake the top 2–3 cm with a clean hand rake or fork to spread mycelium evenly. Disinfect rakes between rooms.
  • Move to pinning (SOP-401) when mycelium shows at the surface over ≈ 70% of the area.

Module 4: Fruiting, Pests, Diseases, Harvesting, & Post-Harvest

Learning outcomes. You will be able to (1) induce uniform pinning with a controlled environmental shock in passive or industrial rooms, (2) run an integrated pest and disease management programme suited to East African highland seasons, (3) harvest and grade to Kenyan retail and international standards, and (4) hold a cold chain from the bed to the buyer's shelf despite power and road constraints.

Linked documents: SOP-401 · SOP-402 · SOP-403 · Deliverable-101 · Deliverable-404

1. Pinning Induction

1.1 The shock sequence

When the casing is colonised (mycelium visible on ≈ 70% of the surface), the room is switched from vegetative to reproductive conditions over 24–48 hours:

Lever From (case run) To (pinning) Mechanism
Air temperature 22–24 °C 16–18 °C Cooling triggers hyphal aggregation into knots
Compost temperature 23–25 °C 17–19 °C Follows the air with a lag; track with probes
CO₂ 3,000–5,000 ppm < 1,000 ppm Removes the inhibition of fruiting
Fresh air Low 4–6 air changes/hour Achieves the CO₂ drop and removes volatile inhibitors
RH 90–95% 85–90% Creates gentle evaporation from the casing, which pins need to form and swell
Watering Building to field capacity Stop or very light for 2–3 days while pins form Pins drown or rot if watered directly

Pins (pinheads) appear in 7–10 days after the shock starts. Detailed procedure and troubleshooting: SOP-401.

1.2 Pinning in a Kenyan passive room (Track K1)

The highland climate supplies the shock for free if you time it:

  • Start the shock on a night with a cool forecast. Open low intakes, earth tubes, and high outlets fully from ≈ 20:00. The building mass gives up its heat overnight and room air falls into the 16–18 °C range by early morning.
  • By day: close or throttle the vents during the afternoon temperature peak; keep the earth-tube fans running (≈ 16–19 °C supply air); run wet hessian curtains in the dry seasons.
  • CO₂ check: use a pressure-compensated meter (or apply the altitude correction from KE-00 §1.4); a raw reading of 800 ppm at Limuru is really ≈ 1,000 ppm.
  • Cold-site variant (Timau, upper Molo, Jun–Aug): night air can fall below 10 °C. Over-cooling below 14 °C slows pinning. Throttle intakes at night and ventilate in late morning instead.

1.3 Pinning in an industrial room (global / Track K2)

The climate controller ramps the air setpoint down by ≈ 1–2 °C per 12 h, steps fresh air up, and holds RH with humidifiers. Typical Dutch practice "airs" the room down to 17–18 °C air and CO₂ ≈ 800–1,000 ppm, then manages the pin count with the evaporation rate.

1.4 Pin-set quality

  • Target: uniform pins spread evenly over the surface at a density that matches the market size (closer spacing gives smaller buttons).
  • Too many pins / clustered pins: excessive or abrupt shock, or casing too wet at the surface. The result is small, crowded mushrooms.
  • Too few pins: shock too weak (CO₂ still > 1,500 ppm, temperature not reaching 18 °C), casing too dry, or poor mycelium in the casing.
  • Pins forming under the casing / stroma: CO₂ too high or casing too deep.

2. Fruiting & Flush Management

Harvest days (first flush) 16–18 °C 82–88% (lower RH dries caps faster, reducing blotch) < 1,000 ppm Water after each day's picking, never just before
Between flushes 17–18 °C 85–90% < 1,200 ppm Heavier watering to restore the casing reservoir (≈ 2–4 L/m² per day over 2–3 days)
  • Flush spacing: flushes come about every 7–10 days. Commercial cropping is normally 3 flushes (sometimes 4). Expected share of total yield: Flush 1 ≈ 45–50%, Flush 2 ≈ 30–35%, Flush 3 ≈ 15–20%. A 4th flush rarely pays its room time and raises disease risk. Terminate and cook out.
  • Watering water: clean, pH 6.5–7.8, low iron. Chlorinated water (see SOP-402) reduces blotch. Borehole water high in bicarbonate slowly raises casing pH (KE-00 §3.4).
  • Cap drying is everything: after each watering, raise air movement so caps are dry within ≈ 2–4 hours. Water films left on caps for longer than ≈ 4 hours are the main trigger of bacterial blotch.

3. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) & Hygiene

3.1 IPM philosophy

Exclusion first, hygiene second, monitoring third, chemicals last. Most pesticide options available to mushroom growers are limited, and Kenyan law permits only PCPB-registered products used according to their label (KE-00 §5). Export buyers apply strict maximum residue limits (MRLs). A farm that controls pests with design, hygiene, and timing is more profitable and more marketable.

3.4 Biosecurity programme

  • Zoning and people flow: clean zone (spawning, spawn run, young crops) → older crops → compost yard. Staff never move backwards in a day. Colour-coded overalls by zone (e.g., white = clean, blue = cropping, green = yard).
  • Entry: footbath (refreshed daily), handwash plus sanitiser, airlock or double door with ≤ 0.6 mm screens.
  • Visitors: no visits to growing rooms from people who have been on another mushroom farm that day; register visitors; provide disposable coats and boot covers.
  • Water: chlorinated or otherwise treated misting and watering water (SOP-402 §4).
  • End of crop: cook-out at 65–70 °C compost temperature for 8–12 h before the spent compost leaves the room. This kills pests and pathogens so they are not spread across the farm. Passive farms without enough steam capacity: seal the room, steam to the maximum achievable and hold longer, then remove the spent compost in covered carts straight to the SMC pad.
  • Spent mushroom compost (SMC): stockpile ≥ 50 m downwind of grow rooms, covered, and sell as a soil conditioner (Module 5).

4. Harvesting Mechanics & Grading

4.1 Harvest timing

  • Harvest before the veil breaks for buttons and cups. Pick daily, and twice daily on peak flush days. A button left 12 hours too long can open into a lower-value flat.
  • Pick in the cool morning (06:00–10:00 in Kenya) so mushrooms enter the cold chain with less field heat.

4.2 Technique

  • Twist-and-pull: grip the cap gently with fingertips, twist, and lift; trim the stem base with a clean, sharp knife over the picking tray, never over the bed. Support adjacent pins.
  • Hygiene: washed hands or clean gloves; trimming knives sanitised; no picking in diseased patches until the end of the round.
  • Bruise prevention: white caps bruise at the lightest pressure. Place, don't drop; pick directly into the final punnet or crate where possible ("pick-to-pack") to avoid re-handling.

4.3 Grading standards

Cup (Grade B) Veil partially stretched, gills just visible 35–55 mm Hotels/restaurants, open-air wholesale, loose retail
Flats / Open (Grade C) Fully open, dark gills 50–90 mm+ Open-air markets, processing (dried slices, powder, soup bases)
Off-spec Bruised, misshapen, discoloured Any Processing only; diseased mushrooms are destroyed, never sold

International reference: UNECE Standard FFV-24 (cultivated mushrooms) defines "Extra/Class I/Class II" quality classes and closed/veiled/open categories. Use it as the specification baseline for supermarket and export agreements. Buyer specs (e.g., size range, stem length, colour) override where stricter.

5. Post-Harvest & Cold Chain

5.1 Cooling targets

  • Mushrooms keep respiring after harvest; at ambient temperature they lose quality within a day. Pull them to 2–4 °C at ≈ 90% RH within ≈ 2–4 hours of harvest.
  • Industrial: vacuum coolers bring the core down in ≈ 15–30 min.
  • Kenyan farm scale: a small insulated cold room (10–20 m³) with an inverter air conditioner and override controller (a well-proven low-cost approach), or a conventional refrigeration unit, plus a fan for forced-air cooling through ventilated crates.

5.2 Kenyan cold-chain realities and workarounds

5.3 Packaging

  • Retail: 200 g or 250 g punnets with micro-perforated stretch film or over-wrap; label with product name, net weight, producer name and address, pack date / best-before, and batch code (see KE-00 §5 and SOP-403).
  • Food service: 2–5 kg ventilated crates lined with paper.
  • Shelf life at 2–4 °C: ≈ 5–7 days for well-cooled white buttons; ≈ 1–2 days if the cold chain is broken.

5.4 Value addition for Grade C and surplus

  • Drying: solar tunnel dryers or electric cabinet dryers at ≤ 50–60 °C; fresh-to-dry ratio ≈ 10:1. Dried slices and powder need a KEBS S-Mark (KE-00 §5).
  • Blanching and brining / pickling, canning: Codex CXS 55 (canned mushrooms) applies; a capital-intensive step for later scale.
  • Economics of processing: Module 5 §3.

Standard Operating Procedure: Pinning Induction & Shock Phase Control (SOP-401)

  • Document ID: SOP-401
  • Version: 2.0
  • Target Region: Global growing-room operations, with Kenyan passive-room procedures
  • Applies To: Climate Control Technicians, Growing Supervisors
  • Linked Documents: Module 4 §1 · SOP-101 · Deliverable-101 · KE-00 §1.4

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP defines the environmental shock that moves a cased room from vegetative growth (case run) to uniform primordia (pinhead) formation, in both controller-managed rooms and passive highland rooms.

2. Start Criteria

  • ☐ Mycelium visible at the casing surface over ≈ 70% of the area
  • ☐ Casing at field capacity (no further heavy watering during the shock)
  • ☐ Room, doors, and screens checked; yellow sticky traps fresh
  • ☐ Weather forecast checked (passive rooms): start on a cool night

3. Shock Parameters

Parameter Start (case run) Target (pinning) Ramp
Air temperature 22–24 °C 16–18 °C Over 24–48 h
Compost temperature 23–25 °C 17–19 °C Follows the air
CO₂ 3,000–5,000 ppm < 1,000 ppm (altitude-corrected) Within 24 h
RH 90–95% 85–90% Within 24 h
Fresh air Low 4–6 air changes/h Step up

4. Procedure

4.1 Controller-managed rooms (global / Track K2)

  1. Step the air setpoint down by 1–2 °C per 12 h until 16–18 °C.
  2. Raise fresh air to bring CO₂ < 1,000 ppm.
  3. Set RH to 85–90%; check that casing evaporation is gentle (surface slightly matt, not shiny-wet, not dry/pale).
  4. Hold for 5–7 days; pins appear about 7–10 days after the shock starts.
  5. Watering: none or very light misting until pins reach pea size (5–8 mm).

4.2 Passive highland rooms (Track K1)

  1. Night 1 (≈ 20:00): open low intakes, earth tubes, and high outlets fully. The building mass dumps its heat overnight.
  2. Morning: record temperature, RH, and CO₂ at 07:00. Target ≤ 18 °C air.
  3. Day: keep earth-tube fans running; throttle main vents during the afternoon peak (11:00–16:00) just enough to hold CO₂ < 1,000 ppm; wet curtains in the dry seasons only.
  4. Nights 2–3: repeat the night flush.
  5. Cold-site variant (Timau / upper Molo / Jun–Aug): if night air falls below 10 °C, throttle the night intakes to stop the room going below 14 °C, and do more of the ventilation in late morning.
  6. Humidity: mist walls/floors (never the casing directly) to hold 85–90%.

4.3 Readings

Every 4–6 h for the first 72 h, then twice daily: air temperature, compost temperature, RH, CO₂ (apply the altitude correction reading × 1,013 / P(site) if the meter is not pressure-compensated; at ≈ 780 hPa multiply by ≈ 1.3).

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP sets out the biosecurity zoning, hygiene routines, water treatment, pest and disease monitoring, outbreak response, post-crop cook-out, and safe chemical handling required to prevent and remediate pest infestations and pathogen outbreaks.

2. Responsibilities

Role Responsibility

3. Safety & Legal

  • Use only PCPB-registered pest control products, applied according to the label: crop, dose, pre-harvest interval (PHI), and re-entry interval. Keep a spray record (date, product, PCPB registration number, dose, room, operator, PHI).
  • Store chemicals in a locked, ventilated store away from food and packaging; keep Safety Data Sheets on file; never decant into drink bottles.
  • PPE per label (gloves, goggles, respirator, overalls). Never mix chlorine products with acids or ammonia (toxic gas).
  • Export and supermarket buyers may require residue testing. Prefer non-chemical controls.

4.2 Entry routine (every entry)

  1. Footbath: disinfectant at the label rate, refreshed daily (more often if soiled; organic matter inactivates disinfectants).

Standard Operating Procedure: Gentle Harvesting, Quality Grading, & Cold-Storage (SOP-403)

  • Document ID: SOP-403
  • Version: 2.0
  • Target Region: Global harvesting and packing rooms, with Kenyan retail, HoReCa, and cold-chain specifics
  • Applies To: Harvesters, Quality Control Inspectors, Packers, Drivers
  • Linked Documents: Module 4 §4–5 · Deliverable-404 · KE-00 §5 (labelling, KEBS, food safety)

1. Purpose & Scope

This SOP defines harvesting technique, timing, grading, packing, labelling, cooling, cold storage, and dispatch so that product reaches buyers at maximum quality and shelf life.

2. Responsibilities

Role Responsibility
Harvest Supervisor Picking plan per room; crew hygiene; yield recording
Harvesters Picking and field grading per this SOP
QC Inspector Grade checks, weight checks, cold-room temperature, release
Packers Packing, labelling, cold storage
Driver Pre-cooled vehicle/coolers, delivery temperature, eTIMS delivery documents

3. Hygiene & Safety

  • Medical certificates for all food handlers (county public health requirement); clean overalls, hair cover, no jewellery; short clean fingernails.
  • Handwashing before starting, after breaks, and after the toilet; gloves where buyer specs require them.
  • Knives sanitised at the start of each room and after diseased patches.
  • Pick diseased areas last and put the waste in lidded bins; never mix with product.
  • Ergonomics: rotate pickers between tiers; use stable platforms for upper tiers (no climbing on shelves).
  • Cold room: inside-release door handle; never lock staff in; limit continuous time in the cold room and provide warm clothing.

4. Procedure

4.1 Picking plan

  • Pick daily, starting at the coolest hour (≈ 06:00), cleanest rooms first (youngest crop → oldest crop).
  • Peak flush days: pick twice daily (morning + early afternoon).
  • Pick mushrooms before the veil stretches for button grade; leave developing pins undisturbed.

4.2 Technique

  1. Grip the cap gently with the fingertips; twist and lift. On dense clusters, support the neighbours with the other hand.
  2. Trim the stem base with a sharp knife over the picking tray, not over the bed (soil and casing on the bed spread disease).
  3. Stem length per buyer spec (typically 10–15 mm below the cap for retail).
  4. Pick-to-pack where possible: harvest Grade A straight into the final punnet or crate to avoid re-handling bruises.
  5. Never throw or drop mushrooms; trays no more than 2–3 layers deep.
  6. Fill holes left in the casing and level disturbed casing after picking.
  7. Water after the picking round, never before.

4.3 Grading standards

Grade Criteria Cap diameter Destination
A — Button Closed cap, veil intact, firm, white/clean; no marks 25–45 mm (buyer spec) Supermarket punnets, premium HoReCa
B — Cup Veil partly stretched/just open; firm; minor marks 35–55 mm HoReCa, wholesale, loose retail
C — Flats/Open Fully open, dark gills, or minor bruising 50–90 mm+ Open-air markets, drying/processing
Reject Diseased (blotch, bubble, cobweb), insect-damaged, slimy Any Destroy in lidded waste; never sell

International reference: UNECE FFV-24 (cultivated mushrooms). The buyer's written specification overrides this table. File a signed spec sheet for every buyer.

4.4 Packing & labelling

  • Pack in the cold room or a cool packing area (≤ 12 °C if possible) with pre-cooled packaging.
  • Retail punnets (200 g / 250 g): check-weigh (minimum declared net weight; no short weights), micro-perforated film.
  • Label (minimum): product name ("Fresh Button Mushrooms Agaricus bisporus"), net weight, producer name and physical address, pack date and best-before (e.g., +5 days at 2–4 °C), batch/room code, storage instruction ("Keep refrigerated at 2–4 °C"), and barcode where the retailer requires it (GS1 Kenya).
  • HoReCa crates (2–5 kg): paper-lined ventilated crates; crate label with grade, weight, and date.

4.5 Cooling & cold storage

  1. Move picked trays to the cold room within 30 minutes of each picking round; never leave trays in the growing room or in the sun.
  2. Cool to 2–4 °C core, ≈ 90% RH within ≈ 2–4 h (industrial vacuum cooling: ≈ 15–30 min).
  3. Forced-air cooling: arrange crates in rows with a fan drawing air through them; do not block evaporator airflow.
  4. Record the cold-room temperature at least 3× daily (or a logger); alarm if > 6 °C.
  5. FIFO: oldest stock dispatched first; maximum cold-store holding ≤ 48 h before dispatch for retail.

4.6 Dispatch

  • Pre-cool the vehicle box or coolers; frozen gel packs wrapped (never touching product).
  • Dispatch early morning; record product temperature at loading and at delivery on the delivery note.
  • Issue an eTIMS invoice for every commercial delivery.
  • Returns/rejections: record the reason; feed the data back into grading and cold-chain reviews.

5. Go-To-Market: Kenyan Channels

5.1 Supermarkets (Naivas, Quickmart, Carrefour, Chandarana FoodPlus, and regional chains)

  • Onboarding: company registration, KRA PIN and tax compliance certificate, eTIMS invoicing, food premises licence, product specification sheet, labelled packaging with barcodes (GS1 Kenya), and a farm audit (some chains use third-party auditors).
  • Commercial terms to negotiate: listing fees, rebates, merchandiser costs, payment terms (commonly 30–60 days or longer), and returns/shrink policy. Avoid sale-or-return arrangements on a 5-day shelf-life product where possible.
  • Concentration risk: the collapses of two large Kenyan retail chains (2017 and 2020–21) left many suppliers unpaid. Cap any single retailer at ≈ 30–40% of receivables, track days-sales-outstanding monthly, and use the protections against abusive buyer practices in Kenya's competition law if payments are unreasonably delayed.
  • Format: 200 g and 250 g punnets of Grade A buttons; brown/cremini as a premium SKU; consistent daily or alternate-day supply to the DC.

5.2 Open-air wholesale markets

  • Nairobi: Wakulima (Marikiti), Muthurwa, City Park, Githurai, Kangemi. Regional: Nakuru, Eldoret, Nyeri, Karatina, Kisumu (Kibuye).
  • Characteristics: cash or M-Pesa on delivery, price volatility, broker margins, little cold chain.
  • Strategy: use for Grade B/C and surplus; build relationships with 2–3 reliable traders; sell early morning; never send product that will sit unrefrigerated for more than a few hours.

5.3 Hospitality & institutions (HoReCa)

  • Nairobi hotels, conference centres, restaurants (Westlands, Kilimani, Karen, CBD), safari lodges and camps (Laikipia/Nanyuki, Naivasha, Maasai Mara via fly-in supply), coastal resorts (by air or reefer), hospitals, schools, and caterers.
  • Value proposition: consistent supply, chef-preferred sizes (cups and browns), 2–5 kg crates, delivery on fixed days, monthly invoicing with eTIMS.
  • December peak: hotels and caterers lift demand from mid-November to early January; plan crops to peak then (KE-00 §1.3).

5.4 Direct-to-consumer (D2C) & digital

  • WhatsApp Business catalogue, Instagram/Facebook, and a simple ordering page; M-Pesa Till/Paybill payment.
  • Weekly subscription boxes (fresh mushrooms + recipe cards) for Nairobi estates; deliveries by motorbike courier in insulated bags.
  • Farm-gate sales and agritourism (farm tours with biosecurity controls, no access to growing rooms).

6. Cooperative & Hub-and-Spoke Economics

The single biggest structural lever for Kenyan scale-up (Module 2 §5):

  • Hub (cooperative/SACCO-owned compost yard + Phase II/III tunnel, ≈ 20–40 t compost per week): CapEx ≈ KES 40–80M, financed through member shares, SACCO lending, AFC/bank term loans, and development grants. The hub sells spawned or Phase III compost to members at cost plus a margin.
  • Spokes: each member builds only cropping rooms (≈ KES 3–6M for 2 × 100 m² passive rooms). No NEMA compost licence and no yard labour at the spoke.
  • Joint marketing: a cooperative brand and a single supermarket supplier code give buyers the volume and consistency they require; one eTIMS-compliant invoicing point.
  • Governance: registered under the Cooperative Societies Act; professional compost manager; quality-based pricing (members are paid by grade).
